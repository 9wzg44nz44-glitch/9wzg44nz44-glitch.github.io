SLW Radar Cross-Section: squat cylinder, sphere, and the multi-layer reflection stack

How strongly does a metal object send a radar signal back? For ordinary radio (transverse EM, “TEM”) textbooks answer this exactly, and we compute it here for a 1 m sphere and a squat 1 m × 1 m cylinder at 433.59 MHz. Then we ask the same question for a scalar-longitudinal wave (SLW). There the honest answer is that no cited source gives a reflection law yet, so the SLW parts are sliders, not predictions.

Educational only. Mainstream classical electrodynamics treats free-space radio as transverse (TEM); scalar-longitudinal waves are disputed. Section A is textbook physics. Sections B–D mix textbook TEM facts with SLW hypotheses; the SLW parts are labelled and use no equation that is not in a cited source.

A · Classical RCS FACT

FACT Radar cross-section (RCS, σ) is the area that, if it caught the incoming power and re-radiated it equally in all directions, would give the same echo the real object gives back toward the radar. It is measured in m² (or dBsm, decibels relative to 1 m²). It depends on size compared with the wavelength, shape, and material (Knott, Shaeffer & Tuley; Ruck Vol. 1 ch. 1–2).

  • FACT Frequency 433.59 MHz (the Monstein & Wesley frequency), so λ = c/f = 0.6914 m.
  • FACT Material: we treat the targets as perfect electric conductors (PEC). Aluminum (σ ≈ 3.5×10⁷ S/m) is effectively PEC at this frequency. Classically its skin depth is only 4.1 µm, far smaller than the 1 m target. Copper or steel hulls give a negligibly different classical RCS for the same reason. (This is classical TEM reasoning, used here only for the classical RCS. It is not applicable under the SLW hypothesis.)
  • FACT The size parameter is ka = 2πa/λ. The conventional regions are Rayleigh (ka ≲ 1: object small, σ ∝ f⁴), resonance / Mie (1 ≲ ka ≲ 10: wiggles from creeping waves), and optical (ka ≳ 10: σ approaches the geometric-optics value). ASSUMPTION The boundaries 1 and 10 are rules of thumb, not sharp lines.

A1 · Sphere, radius a = 1 m

  • FACT ka = 9.09. This is the top of the resonance region, at the transition to the optical region.
  • FACT Optical limit: σ → πa² = 3.14 m² (Knott et al.; Ruck Vol. 1 ch. 3).
  • FACT Exact PEC Mie series, monostatic (backscatter), computed on this page:
    σ = (λ²/4π) · | Σn≥1 (−1)ⁿ (2n+1) (aₙ − bₙ) |² ,   aₙ = ψₙ′(x)/ξₙ′(x),   bₙ = ψₙ(x)/ξₙ(x),   x = ka
    where ψₙ(x) = x·jₙ(x) and ξₙ(x) = x·hₙ⁽¹⁾(x) are Riccati–Bessel functions. These are the Bohren & Huffman (1983, ch. 4, §4.4) scattering coefficients in the perfect-conductor limit (refractive index → ∞), with their backscatter sum (ch. 4; see also Ruck Vol. 1 ch. 3 and Balanis AEE §11.8). Terms are summed to n = x + 4x1/3 + 10.
  • FACT Result at 433.59 MHz: σ = 2.731 m² (4.36 dBsm) = 0.869 × πa², i.e. 0.61 dB below the optical value. The shortfall is the creeping-wave ripple, which is still about ±15 % at ka ≈ 9.
  • FACT The Rayleigh formula σ = 9πa²(ka)⁴ (small-sphere limit, Knott et al.; Ruck Vol. 1 ch. 3) would give 1.9×10⁵ m² here, which is absurd. It is inapplicable at ka = 9; it is only valid for ka ≪ 1.
  • FACT Checks on the series: at ka = 0.009 it agrees with the Rayleigh formula to 2×10⁻⁵. Its maximum is 3.65 πa² at ka ≈ 1.03 (for a = 1 m that is about 50 MHz), which is the familiar textbook peak.

A2 · Squat cylinder, a = 1 m, h = 1 m, broadside

  • FACT Optical broadside formula σ = 2πah²/λ (Knott et al., optics-region formula for a cylinder at broadside) = 9.09 m² (9.58 dBsm).
  • FACT There is no closed-form exact solution for a finite cylinder. It needs a numerical solver (method of moments or similar), which this page does not run.
  • ASSUMPTION Labelled approximation: we take the exact 2-D series for an infinite PEC cylinder (Balanis AEE §11.5.1 TMz, E parallel to the axis; §11.5.2 TEz, H parallel to the axis) and convert with the standard long-body relation quoted by Knott et al.:
    σ2D = (2λ/π) · | Σn≥0 εₙ (−1)ⁿ Jₙ(ka)/Hₙ⁽²⁾(ka) |² (TM; TE uses Jₙ′/Hₙ⁽²⁾′),   ε₀ = 1, εₙ = 2;    σ3D ≈ 2h²σ2D/λ
    The conversion assumes h ≫ λ and ignores the end caps and edge currents. Here h/λ = 1.45, so it is only a rough guide.
  • FACT (arithmetic, given that approximation) σ2D = 3.163 m (1.007 πa) for TM, giving σ3D ≈ 9.15 m² (TM) and 9.25 m² (TE), within 1–2 % of the optical 9.09 m². The Rayleigh (low-frequency) limit is inapplicable at ka = 9.
  • FACT The cylinder's broadside echo is larger than the sphere's (9.1 vs 2.7 m²) because its flat-in-one-direction side focuses the reflection back like a line mirror. Tilt it away from broadside and the echo falls quickly (the sinc pattern in Knott's relation).

A3 · Where the echo comes from (classical)

  • FACT Sphere at ka ≈ 9: the echo is mostly a specular point (the spot facing the radar) plus a smaller creeping wave that travels around the shadow side and returns. Their interference causes the ripple in the plot (Knott et al.; Ruck Vol. 1 ch. 3).
  • FACT Cylinder at broadside: a strong broadside flash from the whole side. It narrows as h/λ grows, with angular width about λ/h.

B · SLW reflection off conductors HYPOTHESIS

What Hively & Loebl, the Erratum and the Hively patent provide on reflection

  • FACT Hively & Loebl, Phys. Essays 32, 112 (2019) give one interface matching condition for the scalar field C: continuity of the normal component of ∇C/μ across an interface, Eq. (26) (derived with a Gaussian pill box, “for long times”). They list it as item 5 of their Table I. They also state that the energy 4πr²(C²/2μ) is constant through a spherical boundary “in arbitrary media” (text around Eq. (25)).
  • FACT They give no reflection coefficient, no transmission coefficient, no angle dependence and no conductor boundary condition for the SLW. One matching condition alone does not fix a reflection law.
  • FACT The 2022 Erratum (Phys. Essays 35, 320) corrects only Appendix A, the no-skin-effect / no-resistive-loss derivation. It says nothing about reflection.
  • FACT Hively US 9,306,527 B1 says qualitatively that “the above tests showed scattering and reflections of the SLW”, “consistent with classical electrodynamics, which predicts scattering of electric fields from conductors due to image charges and image-currents” (col. 14). It attributes the FIG. 6 variability to “scattering and reflections from nearby conductors” (col. 9–10). It proposes “SLW-RADAR” (col. 16; claim 27). It gives no quantitative reflection law.
  • FACT Keyport US 12,525,711 B1 (Navy sleeve/skirt-balun monopole) covers transmitting and receiving SLW, including through water. It contains nothing on reflection or radar.
  • FACT The only measured SLW reflection number is Monstein & Wesley, EPL 59(4), 514 (2002): Earth-surface reflection coefficient ℜ = B²/A² = 0.482 (their Eq. 24) at 433.59 MHz, from the ground-image interference pattern. That is Earth (σ ≈ 10⁻³ S/m), not metal, at near-grazing incidence, and it is one unreplicated experiment.
  • HYPOTHESIS Conclusion: no cited source gives a reflection law or conductor boundary condition for the longitudinal field. We do not derive one. Every SLW reflection value on this page is a user slider.

B1 · Specular or diffuse?

  • FACT Classical, ka ≈ 9: the sphere gives a specular point plus creeping waves; the cylinder gives a broadside flash (Section A3).
  • HYPOTHESIS SLW: unknown. Whether an SLW echo is specular, diffuse, or follows image charges as the patent suggests has not been measured on metal.

C · Submarine reflection stack HYPOTHESIS

HYPOTHESIS Picture an SLW transmitter inside a submarine. It sits in an air gap some distance from the hull, then the signal meets the 76 mm steel pressure hull, then seawater with the surface above and the seabed below, then a target. At every interface some unknown fraction would be reflected (SLW coefficients unknown, so they are sliders). Inside materials, the only SLW loss allowed on this hub is Hively's Joule term.

  • HYPOTHESIS Interface coefficients (air→steel R₁, steel→seawater R₂, seawater→air R₃, seawater→seabed R₄): unknown, no cited law. Sliders default to 0; a button sets them to M&W's 0.482 for illustration only.
  • HYPOTHESIS Loss inside materials, Joule term only (default ON): Hively & Loebl 2019 Eq. (44) contains J·E with J = σE, i.e. Joule heating σE∥². We reuse the hub's Experiment G code unchanged (PR #56, branch expt-g-v0.1, head 726c896): αJ = σH|Z|/2 with Hively's far-field SLW impedance |Z| = η₀/(√ε′·√(1+tan²δ)) (Eq. 37) and σH = ε₀ε″ω. The field falls as e−αJd. ASSUMPTION The step from Eq. (44) to a per-metre αJ is the hub's own construction (Experiment G), not a formula Hively wrote.
  • HYPOTHESIS Erratum toggle: Hively & Loebl's 2022 Erratum says the SLW “is not subject to resistive loss in conductive media” (α = 0). This is available as a labelled toggle. It conflicts with the hub's Experiment C Joule rule, and that conflict is flagged, not resolved.
  • ASSUMPTION Material enters the SLW model only through σ (plus ε′ inside Eq. 37's |Z|). No permeability, skin depth or shielding number is used in the SLW path. For steel we take ε′ = 1. In that good-conductor limit the Joule construction gives αJ → (ω/2c)√ε′ = k₀/2 whatever σ is (a property of the construction, not a Hively statement). At 433.59 MHz that is 4.54 Np/m, so the 76 mm hull costs 3.0 dB.
  • FACT (arithmetic in the model) Seawater σ = 4 S/m at 433.59 MHz: αJ = 35.6 Np/m, so the field falls by e every 2.81 cm (310 dB/m). With the model's σ = 4.29 S/m (15 °C, S = 35) this reproduces Experiment G's αJ exactly (checked in Python).
  • ASSUMPTION Air: σ ≈ 0, so no Joule loss in the air gap. Straight rays with no refraction (no SLW refraction law is known either). Spreading 1/r² in power (H&L 2019 Eq. 38, Table I item 11 HYP). Energy not reflected at an interface is transmitted (bookkeeping; no interface absorption term is cited).

Classical skin depth and cage numbers

transverse-EM reasoning, NOT applicable under the SLW hypothesis

These are ordinary radio (TEM) numbers, shown so you can compare. Under the SLW hypothesis they are not used to rank, limit or dismiss the SLW.

  • FACT (TEM) Skin depth δ = √(2/(ωμσ)) for a good conductor (Jackson 3rd ed. §8.1 and §5.18; Balanis AEE ch. 4). A TEM wave crossing a wall of thickness t falls by about 8.69·t/δ dB. Seawater at 433.59 MHz is only a moderate conductor (tanδ ≈ 2.3), so the exact lossy-medium attenuation is also shown.
  • FACT (TEM) A closed Faraday cage or steel hull strips ordinary TEM noise and signal almost completely. That is exactly why caged receivers are used as TEM controls.
  • HYPOTHESIS An SLW passing through a cage wall or hull is a hypothesis (Hively's 8 GHz copper-box tests, H&L 2019 Fig. 4 and US 9,306,527 FIGs. 3–5, are single-lab results). It is not ruled out or limited by the TEM numbers in this box.

D · Images inside a cavity

  • FACT (TEM) A charge near a flat conductor behaves as if an opposite “image” charge sat behind the surface (method of images, Jackson 3rd ed. ch. 2). Between two parallel conducting walls the images repeat without end, an infinite image series, like facing mirrors.
  • FACT (TEM) A closed metal box is a resonant cavity. Only certain field patterns (modes) at certain frequencies fit, and a source inside drives those modes. The energy bounces around until wall losses (finite Q) absorb it (Jackson 3rd ed. §8.7–8.8). A transmitter inside a submarine hull is, classically, inside a large lossy cavity: nearly all TEM power stays inside as internal multipath.
  • HYPOTHESIS For the SLW, image behaviour inside a closed conductor is untested. The Hively patent says SLW scattering is “consistent with … image charges and image-currents” (col. 14), but gives no measurement inside a cavity. Whether an SLW inside a hull builds up cavity-like internal multipath (large R₁ and R₂) or leaks straight out (R ≈ 0) is exactly what the R sliders in Section C leave open.

E · Conclusion and decisive experiments

  • FACT Classically (TEM, PEC) at 433.59 MHz: 1 m sphere σ = 2.73 m² (exact Mie; 0.87 × πa² = 3.14 m²); 1 m × 1 m cylinder broadside ≈ 9.1 m² (optical 9.09 m²; 2-D→3-D approximation 9.15 m² TM). Both targets sit at ka ≈ 9, the resonance/optical transition. Rayleigh formulas do not apply.
  • HYPOTHESIS An SLW radar off a submarine cannot be designed yet, because no cited source gives an SLW reflection law or boundary condition at a conductor. Hively & Loebl give only the Eq. (26) matching condition, and the only measured SLW reflection is M&W's 0.482 off Earth.
  • HYPOTHESIS With the Joule term ON (default), seawater at 433.59 MHz costs about 310 dB per metre one way, which alone rules out any useful range. With the Erratum toggle (α = 0) only spreading and the unknown reflection coefficients remain. Which reading holds is itself an open experimental question.

Decisive experiments (3–5 that would settle the unknowns)

  1. HYPOTHESIS test SLW reflection off an aluminum plate vs Earth. Use Monstein & Wesley ball antennas at 433.59 MHz. Repeat their ground-image null survey (see the ranging page) over bare ground, then over a large aluminum sheet laid on the ground. Null depth gives B/A. Does ℜ change from 0.482? Include TEM dipole controls at the same geometry.
  2. HYPOTHESIS test Caged-receiver transmission through a steel plate, with TEM controls. Battery receiver sealed in a Faraday cage; transmitter outside. Insert or remove a steel plate (76 mm if possible, thinner plates first) in the path. Controls: a closed-loop (C = 0) antenna, a TEM dipole, and cage-open vs cage-closed runs. This gives R₁R₂-type transmission directly.
  3. HYPOTHESIS test Seawater tank: Joule vs Erratum. Vary salinity and path length in a tank (Experiment G's G2 control e). The Joule reading predicts about 310 dB/m at σ = 4 S/m (433.59 MHz), falling with salinity like σ; the Erratum predicts 1/r only.
  4. HYPOTHESIS test Curved-target glint test. Rotate a sphere and a squat cylinder (Section A sizes, or scaled) in an SLW bistatic setup. A broadside flash that narrows with h/λ would mean SLW scattering follows classical optics; a flat angular response would mean something else.
  5. HYPOTHESIS test Multipath inside a closed metal box. Transmitter and receiver both inside a sealed box. Sweep the frequency and look for cavity-mode peaks at the TEM-predicted frequencies (FACT for TEM, Jackson §8.7) versus an SLW response that ignores them. This measures internal multipath, the “R₁” question.
