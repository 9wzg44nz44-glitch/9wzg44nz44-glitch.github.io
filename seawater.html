Seawater Propagation Tests

A plan for sending and receiving through seawater with coated sphere antennas at 146, 433.92, 1296.1 and about 2449 MHz. It is adapted from a published Russian experiment, without their Tesla coil. All radios and the TinySA receiver stay dry on land or in the boat. Only the coated spheres and their cables go in the water.

Tests of hypotheses, not results. Standard physics predicts very heavy loss in seawater at these frequencies. Nothing here claims a scalar or longitudinal wave has been detected. Terms are explained in the glossary.

1. The paper: Tomilin, Lukin and Gulkov (2021)

A.K. Tomilin, A.F. Lukin and A.N. Gulkov, "An Experiment to Form a Radio Communication Channel in a Marine Environment," Technical Physics Letters 47(8), 577 to 579 (2021). DOI 10.1134/S1063785021060146. The authors are from Tomsk Polytechnic University and Far Eastern Federal University.

Their setup, in our words. The transmit antenna was a 60 mm copper sphere on a metal rod at the center of a flat spiral (Tesla) coil about 320 mm across. A 27.4 MHz CB radio sent an AM tone near 500 Hz at about 1.17 W. The radio and its batteries sat in a sealed metal box fixed to the antenna. The paper says "all antenna elements are insulated with a dielectric layer from contact with seawater", so the sphere had a thin insulating coat with water right against it, not an air pocket. The receiver was built the same way, with a voice recorder in a sealed box. Both were lowered on nylon ropes with no metal parts.

Their test. 27 Oct 2020 in Novik Bay, Vladivostok. Sea depth 13 m, water at +8°C, conductivity 4.77 S/m. The receiver hung at 6 m depth from a moored buoy. The transmitter, at 4 m depth, was towed from 20 m out to about 470 m. They report the tone stayed 5 to 8 dB above noise in a 1 Hz band. They estimate ordinary attenuation at about 195 dB per meter at that frequency. Later work from the same group describes depths of 4 to 10 m.

2. Our adapted design

  • No Tesla coil. Dr. Hively's view is that the coil and rod add nothing important. The key is a sphere driven by an electric field.
  • Radios topside. Unlike Tomilin, every radio and the TinySA receiver stay dry on land or in the boat. A coax wet run goes down to each sphere.
  • Coated sphere in water contact. Like Tomilin, each sphere gets a thin even coat of epoxy with seawater touching the coat. No air pocket, because a sphere in air inside a box acts like an ordinary antenna in a box.
  • Potted feed, no tube by default. The sphere, its feed stub and sleeve, and the end of the coax are cast into one solid block of epoxy. A small Blue Robotics acrylic tube is an optional feed housing only if a potted block proves hard to make watertight.
  • No Nanuk cases in the water. They are rated IP67, only 1 m for 30 minutes.

Sleeve lengths are for air. Epoxy and seawater against the coat will detune the stub a lot. We have not found a verified dielectric constant for the epoxy at these frequencies, so it must be measured. The stub and balun set the tuning, not the sphere size, so the 2449 MHz sphere is larger than 0.08 wavelength on purpose. Aluminum does not solder easily: attach the stub mechanically (a threaded stub, a crimp or a clamp), or use the Experiment A method on exp-1296.html (aluminum solder with aluminum flux, or silver conductive epoxy). Retune with the VNA (see the VNA how-to) first in the tank, then again in the sea.

4. Wet cable, potting and chokes

  • Wet run: RG-400. Solid PTFE dielectric and FEP jacket, 4.95 mm across. Foam cables such as LMR-240UF and LMR-400UF can wick water, so use them only for dry runs in the boat. RG-213 is solid PE but about 10.3 mm across, too big for a WetLink penetrator (maximum 9.5 mm).
  • Potting. MG Chemicals 832WC epoxy. Pot the coax end, connector and stub into one block so water has no path along the jacket.
  • Penetrator (optional tube only). WetLink M10 5.5 mm HC fits 5.0 ± 0.3 mm cable. WetLink's 1000 m rating is for their tested cables, and FEP jacket is not one of them, so pot over the penetrator too.
  • Keep wet runs short at 1296 and 2449 MHz, since coax loss rises fast with frequency.
  • Waterline choke. Stops RF current running back up the outside of the coax to the boat. Clamp-on ferrites at 146 and 433 MHz; a quarter-wave sleeve choke at 1.3 and 2.4 GHz.
  • Rope and weights. Hang each sphere on nylon rope with a weight and no metal in the line, as Tomilin did. Tie the coax to the rope about every meter for strain relief, with a drip loop at the boat.

5. Depth, pressure and leaks

  • Pressure rises about 1 bar for every 10 m of water.
  • Target 10 m, the deepest the Tomilin group reports. Build for 30 m (about 4 bar) for margin.
  • Maximum time under water is one week. The 3 in acrylic 240 mm tube is rated 150 m for submersions under two weeks, so it is fine if used.
  • Proof test every potted block before it carries RF: at 1.5 times the target pressure in a pressure pot, or lowered empty to 15 m or more on the rope.
  • Leak checks. For a potted block, check insulation from the sphere and coax shield to the water with a meter, and watch for a jump in SWR. If the optional tube is used, fit a Blue Robotics SOS leak sensor inside.

6. Corrosion

No bare metal touches the water. All four spheres are now 3003 aluminum, while the SMA bulkhead and coax are brass and copper. Keep that joint fully sealed inside the potting so seawater never bridges the two metals. Rinse everything in fresh water after each session and check the coat for chips.

7. Test sequence: tank first

  1. Air. Tune each sphere on the bench with the VNA and record SWR.
  2. Fresh water in the 110 gal tank. Retune and record.
  3. Seawater in the tank (Instant Ocean, checked with the EC meter and hydrometer). Retune and record.
  4. Sea. Shallow first, then step down toward 10 m. Retune at depth.

Run the seawater null test on long-distance.html before any sea session.

9. Compliance and exposure

  • All amateur transmissions identify as KC1CZU. Power limits, keep-back distances and worker rules are on long-distance.html (see also RF exposure for workers).
  • Boat crew. The topside feedline and the waterline choke can carry RF. Keep the crew back from both while transmitting, using the keep-back tables on the long-distance page, and run the coax away from where people sit.
  • GMRS radios only unmodified, with their own antennas. They must never feed the spheres. Sphere tests use amateur radios and the TinySA only.
