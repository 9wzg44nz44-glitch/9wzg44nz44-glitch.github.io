Range finder and direction-finding dipoles

How far apart are the transmitter and the receiver, and in which direction is the transmitter? This page defines the notation, says which parts are ordinary validated physics and which are hypotheses, and gives an in-browser calculator that turns VNA sweeps, null positions and dipole rotations into a range with an uncertainty. It works for the three sphere benches and, with limits, for the microwave oven.

Tests of hypotheses, not results. Mainstream electrodynamics treats free-space radio as transverse (TEM). A longitudinal or scalar component is a hypothesis we are testing, and nothing on these pages claims that scalar or longitudinal waves have been detected. Values marked proposal are our suggestions and are not measured or sourced. Draft v0.1, 4 Oct 2026, written by Grok Bot for Dan Britton.

1. Notation

  • r: straight-line distance from the TX sphere centre to the RX sphere centre (the "range"). This is the hub's usual meaning (setup.html "range r is sphere-to-sphere").
  • x: horizontal ground distance between TX and RX, used only in the two-ray (ground-reflection) model, as in Monstein and Wesley (2002). With heights h1, h2: r = √(x² + (h2 - h1)²).
  • λ = c/f in free space, c = 299 792 458 m/s. The S21 phase is simply called "phase" here, in degrees; φ is reserved for the rod angle on the Joule-loss page.

2. The idea, read honestly

Monstein and Wesley saw signal minima at several distances along a street (39 m and 23 m confirmed, others "probably exist"). Their own model explains them as two-path interference: the direct wave plus a wave reflected from the ground. Null positions depend only on λ and the two antenna heights (see the ranging page; for their geometry the model gives 119.2, 39.3, 23.0, 15.8 m). That is ordinary multipath, and it is useful: it means a TX and RX pair over a reflecting surface carries distance information with no extra hardware.

So Dan's reading, that an "automatic range finder" is built into the Experiment B setup, is reasonable as an engineering idea, with one correction: the nulls are not SLW-specific, so seeing them says nothing about longitudinal waves. What we can test is whether the information can be read out automatically and reliably at 433.59 MHz, 1296 MHz and 2450 MHz, on the sphere benches and at the oven. The same VNA sweep we need anyway for the rod baselines also holds phase slope and ripple, so the readout can be a button.

Improvements on the first idea.

  1. Do not walk the receiver; sweep frequency. The null pattern moves with frequency, so the ripple in |S21| versus f gives the path difference without moving anything.
  2. Phase slope. Over a clean single path, S21 phase falls by 360° every c/r in frequency, so r = -(d phase/df) c/360. Two frequencies resolve it coarsely; one frequency refines it to a fraction of λ.
  3. Time-domain gating. A Hann-windowed inverse transform of the complex S21 shows the direct path and the ground bounce as separate peaks when they are farther apart than about c/B.
  4. Self-consistency test for null series. A real two-ray series has 1/xn equally spaced; the calculator reports whether it is.
  5. Triangulation. Two dipole bearings give r with no multipath assumption (and work on the oven, which has no coherent reference).
  6. Tape-measure calibration table with a plot of estimate versus tape, so the claim "it works" is a number.

6. Dipole direction-finding antennas, one per band

The Dipole Detector is a centre-fed brass-rod receiving dipole (1/8 in, 1.5 mm feed gap, SMA edge-mount jack, quarter-wave sleeve balun, non-conductive plate), described on the build sheet page. It is a receiving antenna, not one of the polarising rods; keep it separate from the rod arrays. First-pass lengths (cut long and trim):

How it finds direction. A dipole's received power follows cos² of the angle between its axis and the E-field direction. Rotate it on a turntable, fit the figure-eight, and the maximum (or the null) gives an axis direction.

  • fact For a transverse wave with E along the dipole, the maximum is broadside to the source and the null is end-on. A null is sharper than a peak, so it makes the better pointer.
  • hypothesis For a wave with an E component along the propagation direction (the SLW claim), the maximum would be end-on instead. So the same rotation is also a polarisation test: compare the fitted peak with the known source bearing.
  • caution Close to any small antenna (inside roughly λ/(2π) and not far beyond) an ordinary field also has a radial E component, so an end-on peak near the TX proves nothing. The hub's near-field plan advises keeping detectors several wavelengths out. Look at how the end-on part changes with range.
  • Bearing has a 180° ambiguity; resolve it by moving the antenna or by a second station. Calibrate on a source at a known bearing first, with the TX feed axis set so the E direction is known.
  • Check that the sleeve works (slide test in vna-howto.html section 7). Coax carrying common-mode current bends the pattern and fakes bearings.

Procedure (proposal). Dipole horizontal on a turntable with a 1° scale; TX feed axis horizontal and perpendicular to the line; rotate in 15° steps over 360° (the second half is a free symmetry check), log the level at each step with the VNA at one CW frequency or the TinySA in peak hold; fit; repeat at three ranges; rotate the TX sphere 90° and repeat.

7. Calibrate against a tape, and how to falsify

  1. Before looking at data, write down thresholds (for example a maximum allowed error as a fraction of λ or as centimetres) in the To-Do list. These are yours to choose; this page does not set them.
  2. For each band, measure at least five ranges with a tape or laser, centre to centre, and run the estimate each time.
  3. Fit estimate = slope × tape + offset. A constant offset is a phase-centre or cable correction. A slope that is not 1, or scatter above the quoted error, fails the method on that band.
  4. Repeat with the rods in and out. If the rods move the range estimate, the rods are changing the propagation path, which is itself interesting.
  5. Repeat on another day and in another place. Two-ray results should move when you change a height; if they do not, the ripple is not from the ground.

The oven. The oven gives no coherent reference, so VNA phase methods do not apply (and the VNA must never be connected to the oven antenna or cavity). Use amplitude nulls along a line, the dipole bearings with triangulation, and the tape. The magnetron's frequency drifts, so ripple-in-frequency is not available from the oven alone.
