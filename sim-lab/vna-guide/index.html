How to read the labels. FACT = stated in the named source (page or figure given). HYP = a hypothesis (for example an author's reading of what a signal means). Not established physics. IDEA = our suggestion for the bench; it is not from a source. NOT STATED = the source does not say. We do not guess. Numbers on this page are copied from the sources or from the maker's manual and data sheet; the only arithmetic we add is marked "our arithmetic".

1. What a VNA is, and why we use it

A VNA (vector network analyzer) is a test box for radio parts. It sends a small, known radio signal into a part and measures what happens to it. Think of it as a sound-and-echo tester:

  • What bounces back (the echo) tells you how well the part is matched to the 50 Ω cable. That is called S11.
  • What comes out the far end tells you how much signal the part lets through. That is called S21.
  • "Vector" means it measures both the size of the signal (in dB) and its timing (phase), at many frequencies in one sweep.

Why we use it. Our experiments hunt very small signals. Before a run it helps to know that each antenna feed (sphere, stub, sleeve balun), cable and filter behaves the way you think at the frequency you care about. IDEA A bad connector or a mismatched feed can add or hide signal, and a VNA shows that in seconds.

How it differs from the TinySA Ultra. The TinySA is a spectrum analyzer: it listens to what is in the air or on a cable. The VNA tests a part: it sends its own signal and measures how the part passes or reflects it. The hub's kit page says the same: the TinySA is the on-air listener and generator, the VNA is the characterization tool (kit.html#vna6000).

Words you will see (plain meanings)

DUT
Device under test: the part you are measuring (a sphere feed, a cable, a filter).
Port
An SMA connector on the VNA. The VNA6000 has two. Port 1 does the echo measurement (S11). Port 2 receives what passes through (S21). The manual calls them CH0 (reflection) and CH1 (through) (manual p.16).
50 Ω
The standard "size" of the cable and the ports. A part that is not 50 Ω at the test frequency sends some of the signal back as an echo.
dB (decibel)
A way to count ratios. Every 10 dB is a factor of 10 in power: −10 dB is one tenth, −20 dB is one hundredth, −3 dB is about half. A minus sign means "less than what went in".
S11 (reflection)
How much of the test signal comes back from the part, in dB. 0 dB means all of it comes back (nothing absorbed or radiated). The more negative, the better the match.
Return loss
The same echo measurement quoted as a positive number: a return loss of 20 dB is an S11 of −20 dB. Bigger is better.
SWR
Standing wave ratio: another way to say how big the echo is. 1.0 is perfect; bigger is worse. Radio people quote it for antennas.
S21 (transmission)
How much of the signal gets from port 1 through the part to port 2, in dB. 0 dB means all of it. −3 dB means about half. Often called insertion loss when the part is a filter or cable.
Smith chart
A round chart that plots a part's impedance (resistance plus reactance) as a dot. The centre is a perfect 50 Ω match; the outer edge is total echo. You can ignore it at first: the dB and SWR views are enough to start.
Sweep
The VNA steps from a start frequency to a stop frequency and plots the result across the whole range.
Calibration (open, short, load, thru)
Measuring parts whose behaviour is known so the VNA can subtract its own cable and connector errors. The place where you calibrate is the "reference plane", the spot the VNA treats as zero.

First session in six lines (details in tab A)

  1. Put the two SMA connector savers on the two ports and leave them on.
  2. Open the menu (tap an empty spot on the screen) and set the sweep: STIMULUS → START and STOP.
  3. Calibrate at the end of the cable you will measure through: CAL → RESET → CALIBRATE, then OPEN, SHORT, LOAD, DONE.
  4. Connect the part. Show S11 as LOGMAG or SWR.
  5. Move a marker to the dip (the best match) and write down the frequency and the dB.
  6. Save the calibration (CAL → SAVE) and photograph the screen for your log.
Three rules before you plug anything in.
  1. Limits. Never put more than +10 dBm (10 mW) of RF, or more than 5 V DC, into either port (manual p.3; data sheet p.2). Do not connect a transmitter, an amplifier output or a DC-biased tube straight to a port. IDEA If you are unsure of a source's level, put a fixed attenuator in line first.
  2. The Faraday cage. No cable crosses the cage wall (Dan's standing rule). VNA measurements of parts happen outside the cage. Inside, only a battery-powered VNA with the USB cable unplugged, and never during a real listen: the VNA sends its own test signal (typically −10 dBm, data sheet p.3), which is far stronger than the faint signals we hunt. For the framing: the cage strips ordinary transverse-EM (TEM) noise (FACT); whether an SLW passes the cage wall is HYP. A VNA reading of a part does not test that either way.
  3. Test signal on an antenna. A VNA sweep sends its test signal across the whole range you set. The hub says on-air work at 23 cm and 70 cm needs a licence. IDEA With an antenna connected, keep the sweep narrow and the level low, or use a dummy load, and check your local rules. This page is not legal advice.

What others measured with a VNA

Short answer. Dan's hunch is right: the Zimmerman papers are the ones where a VNA is the main instrument, and S11 and S21 are the data. In our library that is three papers: Nikolova & Zimmerman (2007), Zimmerman's Modern Physics Letters B paper (2011) and his Journal of Applied Physics paper (2013). Zimmerman's QEX article is a how-to that talks about return loss and SWR but reports no VNA measurement. Hively's patent names a VNA model and the 2019 Hively & Loebl paper plots S21 and S12, but that paper's text does not name the instrument. Monstein & Wesley (2002) and Butterworth et al. (2013) did not use a VNA (a log detector with a voltmeter, and a USB spectrum analyzer).

Details by paper

Nikolova & Zimmerman 2007 (CEM-R-46): VNA used, S21 and return loss
  • FACT Setup (Fig. 3, PDF p.19). A transmit folded dipole, a propagation path, a fluorescent U-tube detector, a bias-tee, a current-regulated supply, a 30 dB amplifier in the RF path, and a vector network analyzer. A 50 Ω coax carries both the RF to the VNA port and the DC bias, separated by the bias-tee (PDF p.21).
  • FACT Instrument. "an Advantest VNA R3765CG" (PDF p.21). VNA output power "set at 5 dBm" (PDF p.20).
  • FACT What they did with it. Measured the complex S21 with a positive and then a negative DC bias, and formed the sum and difference: S_A = 0.5 (S21+ − S21−) and S(E,H) = 0.5 (S21+ + S21−) (Eq. 70, PDF p.22). They state that a VNA is "a phase-coherent system" that allows this.
  • FACT Frequencies and results. 1.1 to 1.5 GHz. In a compact anechoic chamber at 1.2 to 1.5 m with no amplifier, the transmitting monopole's "return loss is minimum at 1.22 GHz and is about −16 dB" (PDF p.23). Magnitude and phase of S21 at 1.2 m are in Figs. 7 and 8 (PDF p.23 to 24). The measured S_A at 1.3 GHz and 1.2 m is −59.7 dB against a calculated −56.7 dB (PDF p.24).
  • FACT In the open. A folded dipole fed through a 4:1 balun, "reflection loss is −15 dB at about 1.21 GHz", with an amplifier of 29 to 30 dB gain and data every 25 cm (Fig. 10, PDF pp.25 to 26).
  • FACT Balun remark. Power-type signals "may be picked up by the feed coaxial lines … if the feed baluns are not well tuned" (PDF p.22). That is the reason to check a balun with a VNA.
  • FACT Their estimate for 3 m is an S21 of about −65 dB, "well above the noise level of the system" (PDF p.21).
  • HYP The authors read the bias-dependent part of S21 as a "potential signal" (vector potential). That interpretation is theirs and is not settled physics.
Zimmerman 2011 (MPLB): VNA used, S11 and S21 across 1280 to 1380 MHz
  • FACT Setup (Fig. 1, p.650). A 3 m long, 17.78 cm diameter circular copper waveguide excited by a centred coaxial probe at the head. A folded mercury-vapour plasma tube (a compact fluorescent lamp) at the far end is the detector. VNA port 1 feeds the probe. The tube connects through a bias-tee to VNA port 2 (p.650). The DC bias polarity is reversed with a switch.
  • FACT Calibration. "The experimental data are taken with a full 2-port calibration of the VNA and cables with reference loads, shorts, and opens traceable to NIST" (p.653).
  • FACT What was plotted (Fig. 5a, p.654). |S11|² at the top, the calculated (1 − |S11|²) below it, and |S21|² at the bottom for both bias polarities, 1280 to 1380 MHz. When losses are present, (1 − |S11|²) exceeds |S21|². The negative-bias S21 has a mean of −16.5 dB ± 1.0 dB; the positive-bias curve is some 5 dB lower; both S21 curves are anti-correlated with (1 − |S11|²) (p.653).
  • FACT Control. A copper mock-up of the tube was measured instead (Fig. 5b, 5c): its S21 does not split with bias current and tracks (1 − |S11|²) (p.653). This is a good habit: swap in a dummy and see what the VNA shows.
  • FACT Free-space tests. Open-ended waveguides at 1.5, 6 and 25 m with phase data; the VNA output was +10 dBm for the waveguide test and the 1.5 m test, and an external amplifier at +20 dBm was used for 6 and 25 m (pp.655 to 658). The conclusion lists that A and E "may be individually calculated from amplitude/phase data collected with a VNA" (Eqs. 3a, 3b; p.658).
  • FACT A bias-tee used here had "return loss … < −25 dB from 1 GHz to 1.48 GHz" and an RF insertion loss of −0.04 dB (footnote, p.650).
  • HYP The split into Az and Ez components, and the Aharonov-Bohm reading, are the author's interpretation.
  • Copy note. The hub's library copy of this paper carries the publisher's "re-use … strictly not permitted" stamp, so this guide links the publisher's DOI page and not the PDF.
Zimmerman 2013 (J. Appl. Phys.): network analyzer used, S21 magnitude and phase at 1300 MHz
  • FACT Setup (Figs. 1 to 3, pp.044907-2 and -3). A transmitting monopole with a quarter-wave-radius reflector at 1300 MHz, aimed at an NE-2 neon bulb 2 m away. DC bias reaches the bulb through a bias-tee with 0.04 dB insertion loss and more than 25 dB isolation.
  • FACT How the VNA was used. "The monopole is driven with 1 mW of RF power (0 dBm). With this power level as a reference, the network analyzer display may be read directly in dBm" (p.044907-3).
  • FACT Fig. 4: S21 for both bias polarities (bias current 8.8 mA). The two traces differ by more than 10 dB at the centre frequency; the mean received power is −52 dBm (p.044907-3).
  • FACT Fig. 5: phase. The RF phase reverses when the bias current is reversed. The plot is 360 degrees per division, centre 1300 MHz, span 50 MHz, with "a port extension of 73 ns" on port 1 to "unwind" the phase (p.044907-3).
  • FACT Fig. 7: the gain antenna. With the bulb a quarter wave in front of a planar reflector, the VNA S21 plot (Fig. 7) shows about a 6 dB improvement (p.044907-4).
  • FACT The paper says signal levels were "confirmed for R = 2, 50, and 100 meters" (p.044907-4). Which instrument was used at 50 and 100 m is NOT STATED.
  • FACT A worked mismatch example (not a VNA reading): a bulb of 8522 Ω on a 50 Ω cable has a voltage reflection coefficient of 0.9885 (Eq. 14, p.044907-2). That is the same idea as S11, taken to the extreme.
  • HYP The paper reads the phase reversal as "a cardinal sign of vector potential detection". That is the author's claim.
  • Copy note. AIP's copyright notice is stamped on each page, so this guide links the publisher's DOI page and not the hub copy.
Zimmerman 2011 (QEX): a how-to, no VNA measurement
  • FACT A 3 dB pad on the coax to the waveguide "will provide a return loss of 6 dB for an SWR of 3" (p.32). The text warns that a zero-ohm load "will yield an infinite SWR" on the transmitter's cable, so a pad (or a circulator) protects the transmitter (p.32).
  • FACT The lamp tube carries the received current "similar to a parallel transmission line" out to a coax and on to the receiver (p.31). The MPLB paper puts a number on that line: 125 Ω, against the 50 Ω cable, so the mismatch reflection is (50 − 125)/(50 + 125) = −0.43 (MPLB p.653).
  • FACT A bias-tee design at 1296 MHz built on a 50 Ω quarter-wave stub with two 1000 pF capacitors, one to block DC from the receiver line and one to bypass the stub's cold end (p.34). About −400 V DC is momentarily needed to ignite the tube (p.33). The text says the report is based on the MPLB paper.
  • No S-parameter measurement is reported. Scanned copy; read by OCR.
Works & Works 2012 (QEX): no VNA
  • FACT Test range at 1296.1 MHz with antenna mounts 15.2 m apart; a Flex-3000 SDR receiver with S-meter averaging; 21.6 dBm transmit power available; over 500 measurements; EZNEC models (PDF pp.1 to 2).
  • FACT "An isolator at the transmit mount provides low SWR on the transmit feed line" (PDF p.1). No VNA or S11 is reported.
Monstein & Wesley 2002 and Butterworth et al. 2013: no VNA
  • FACT M&W 2002: the rod-cube test (Fig. 3) is a measured transmission coefficient T at 433.59 MHz against the angle φ, with the curve T = exp[−κ cos²φ], κ = 4.4 (Eq. 9; pp.516 to 517). The instrument for Fig. 3 is NOT STATED.
  • FACT M&W 2002 Rhine test: a Trio TR-9500 transmitter on a 12 V car battery, triggered by a 1 s oscillator so each cycle includes a quiet reference; receiver = ball antenna + LNA435 low-noise amplifier + AD8307 logarithmic RF detector + digital voltmeter, with a PICO-ADC10 analog-to-digital converter to a PC and GPS positions (p.518).
  • FACT Butterworth et al. 2013: Realistic HTX-404 UHF transceiver at 446 MHz; received signal "analyzed using a Signal Hound USB-SA44B Signal Analyzer" (p.16). They did not see the M&W polarizer null with ball antennas; the arrays did act as polarizers with simple half-wave dipoles (p.17).
  • IDEA Our arithmetic from M&W's own formula: at φ = 0 the model gives T = e^(−4.4) = 0.0123, which is −19.1 dB relative to no loss. That is the size of drop to look for if you repeat the rod-cube test with the VNA (see the Experiments tab). Butterworth's result is a reason to keep a dipole-antenna control.
  • Hively & Loebl 2019 (Appendix D, pp.124 to 125) prefer a network analyzer's phase-locked signal to M&W's custom transmitter and receiver. That is their view of the method (HYP).
Hively patent (US 9,306,527 B1) and Hively & Loebl 2019: S-parameters at 8 GHz
  • FACT Patent, col. 7 (PDF p.14). "Some implementations may include an Agilent Technologies E5071C network analyzer (300 kHz to 20 GHz)." Transmitting and receiving antennas "may be identical, because the reciprocity theorem guarantees" the transmitter can also act as a receiver. This VNA is a bench instrument; Dan's VNA6000-A tops out at 6 GHz, below the 8 GHz these tests used.
  • FACT Balun numbers as printed. Patent col. 7: "The measured return loss was −22 dB for a single skirt balun … and −42 dB for a double balun (not shown)", and an antenna-balun impedance of 49.76 − 0.24j Ω against the 50 Ω source. The 2019 paper (p.118 and Fig. 3, p.120) shows the measured balun return-current attenuation with a shallow null of 23 dB at 8.00 GHz, against a simulated null of 49 dB at 7.94 GHz, and gives 42 dB for a double balun (not shown). The two documents state this differently (22 versus 23 dB). HYP They may describe the same measurement; we have not reconciled them.
  • FACT Attenuation versus distance. Patent cols. 9 to 10 and 2019 Fig. 4 (p.120) show two facing antennas at 8.00 GHz, each inside its own copper pipe enclosure, inside an anechoic Faraday chamber ("90 dB isolation", p.119). The patent labels the antennas "port-1" and "port-2"; the paper labels the plots S21 and S12. The instrument is not named in the paper's text. The test layout uses "directional couplers" described as 45 dB isolators (p.118).
  • FACT The paper reports "an attenuation between 115 dB at r = 2 cm separation and 137 dB at r = 30 cm" for that test (p.120). Any comparison with an ordinary skin-depth shielding estimate is transverse-EM reasoning, NOT applicable under the SLW hypothesis, so this guide makes none.
  • HYP Whether the SLW passes the enclosures is the paper's claim, not an established result.
What the VNA papers teach us for our bench
  1. FACT They measured S11 and S21 together and compared them: (1 − |S11|²) against |S21|² (MPLB Fig. 5). IDEA For each antenna pair, record both.
  2. FACT They calibrated with the cables on (MPLB p.653) and used a port extension to straighten out phase (JAP Fig. 5). The VNA6000 manual says a saved calibration also keeps the "electrical delay" setting (manual p.22).
  3. FACT They used a dummy (a copper stand-in for the plasma tube) as a control (MPLB Fig. 5b, 5c).
  4. FACT Their drive levels were higher than Dan's VNA: +10 dBm (MPLB), 5 dBm (CEM-R-46), 0 dBm (JAP), against a VNA6000 test-port output of about −10 dBm typical (data sheet p.3). Our arithmetic: that is 10 to 20 dB lower. The S21 reading in dB does not depend on the drive level, but the noise floor eats more of a weak signal. The data sheet gives a 95 dB transmission dynamic range for the VNA6000-A at 200 MHz to 5.8 GHz (data sheet p.4, measured with IF bandwidth 0.8 kHz and 10 averages), so S21 values of −45 to −70 dB, as in CEM-R-46, should sit above the floor (our arithmetic, not a test). IDEA Use a narrow IF bandwidth or averaging on weak S21 readings.
  5. FACT DC bias needs a bias-tee. All three Zimmerman VNA papers feed the DC bias to the plasma tube or bulb through a bias-tee. The VNA6000 ports take at most 5 V DC (data sheet p.2), and Zimmerman's QEX article says about −400 V DC is momentarily needed to ignite the tube (p.33). Never connect a biased tube straight to a VNA port.

What to measure on Experiments A, B and C

Everything in the recipes below is an IDEA of ours: a way to apply the VNA to the hub's kit, patterned on what the sources did. The hub's kit page lists these same uses (S11 of the sphere feed, S21 of the 23 cm filter, a TDR check of cable pigtails, an optional stub S11). Keep the sources' HYP framing: a good S11 only tells you a part is a decent 50 Ω match; it does not test the SLW hypothesis.

Recipe 1: S11 of a sphere and its feed (Experiments A and B)

  1. What it tells you: how much of the test signal the ball and its feed send back. A dip at your frequency means the ball accepts power there. A ball with a short-circuited or open feed shows almost 0 dB everywhere. IDEA
  2. Calibrate at the end of the cable you will use (see the Step-by-step tab), with the sweep window from the table above.
  3. Connect the sphere's SMA bulkhead to the cable end. Show S11 as LOGMAG and again as SWR.
  4. Touch test: hold the ball by hand and by its stand, and move the cable. A reading that jumps means the outer conductor of the cable is carrying signal. That is the same worry N&Z (2007, PDF p.22) raise about badly tuned feed baluns. IDEA
  5. Control: repeat with the ball replaced by a 50 Ω load (a dummy), as Zimmerman did with his copper stand-in (MPLB Fig. 5b, 5c). If the readings look alike, the ball is not telling you anything yet.
  6. Log: frequency of the dip, depth in dB, SWR at your operating frequency, the cable, and a photo of the screen.

Recipe 2: the sphere with its stub or sleeve balun (Experiment A, C)

  1. Measure the sphere alone (Recipe 1), then the sphere with the sleeve stub / sleeve balun fitted, with the same calibration. Overlay the two saved traces.
  2. What to look for: whether the match moves and whether the cable-touch jump goes away. A sleeve balun's job is to keep signal off the outside of the coax. IDEA See the hub's sleeve balun page for the geometry.
  3. Source analogs: Hively's patent reports a balun return loss of −22 dB (single) and −42 dB (double) at 8 GHz (col. 7; FACT as printed), and N&Z measured a folded dipole with a 4:1 balun at −15 dB at about 1.21 GHz (PDF p.25). Neither number is a target for your hardware; they only show what the others measured.

Recipe 3: cables, pigtails and connectors

  1. Cable loss (S21): connect the cable between port 1 and port 2 (Thru calibrated). Read S21 at your frequency. A good short jumper reads a small negative number of dB. A sudden worse reading usually means a bad crimp or connector.
  2. Cable fault finding (time domain): the manual covers a time-domain (TDR) mode that shows reflections against distance (manual pp.18 to 21). Enter the cable's velocity factor as a whole number such as 67, not 0.67 (manual pp.18 to 21). Use the RG-316 data sheet value for your cable. The hub's kit page lists this as a use of the VNA.
  3. Hints: SMA connectors want a snug finger-tight turn, then a gentle nudge with an SMA wrench (do not over-tighten and do not twist the cable). Keep the cable still while the sweep runs, and keep the same cable from calibration to measurement. Use the supplied connector savers on the VNA ports, so wear happens on a cheap adapter. Avoid tight bends in the RG-316. IDEA (manual p.4 introduces the connector savers; the torque and handling hints are our general practice, not from the manual).

Recipe 4: the 23 cm band-pass filter (Experiment A) — S21

  1. Calibrate both ports with the Thru, sweep 1000 to 1600 MHz.
  2. Connect the filter between port 1 and port 2. Show S21 as LOGMAG.
  3. Read the pass band: the loss at 1296 MHz, and how fast it drops each side. The filter's data sheet gives the maker's expected curve; compare. IDEA
  4. This is the same kind of measurement as the low-pass filter video in the Links tab.

Recipe 5: the rod polarizer cube (Experiment B) — S21 and loading

The hub says the polarizer cube is not by itself a two-port part, so the following are our suggestions, not something a source did with a VNA. (Monstein & Wesley did not use a VNA; their measurement instrument for the cube curve is not stated.)

  1. Path S21: put the transmit ball on port 1 and the receive ball on port 2, with the cube between them at a fixed spacing. Calibrate with a Thru at the cable ends. Record S21 at 433.59 MHz at each cube rotation angle φ. IDEA
  2. What to compare: M&W's formula gives T = exp[−4.4 cos²φ] (Eq. 9, p.516); our arithmetic: that is a 19.1 dB drop at φ = 0. Butterworth et al. (2013) did not see that null with ball antennas but did with half-wave dipoles (p.17). Whatever you see, log it as measured, and say which antennas were used.
  3. Loading S11: measure the transmit ball's S11 with the cube removed, then in place at each angle, to see whether the cube pulls the match around. IDEA
  4. Keep the room fixed. People moving, metal tables and the cables will change an air path S21 by several dB. Do all angles in one sitting with the cables taped down.
  5. Rule: This is a bench job outside the cage, with the VNA at a low sweep rate. The cage itself is not on this list.

The cage rule, again

  • No cable crosses the Faraday cage wall (Dan's standing rule). Parts are measured outside. If a measurement must be done inside, it is battery or optical only, with no cable through the wall, and the VNA off during any real listen.
  • Cage framing: it strips ordinary transverse-EM noise (FACT); whether an SLW passes the wall is HYP. The hub's submarine page lists a "seam-coupon S21 with the NanoRFE VNA outside the cage" (tagged transverse-EM reasoning); keep that tag if you do it.
  • Under the SLW hypothesis we do not argue from skin depth or permeability. Any such reasoning carries the tag "transverse-EM reasoning, NOT applicable under the SLW hypothesis".

What a VNA in air cannot tell you about the Joule term

The hub's loss-modes page (sim-lab/loss-modes) compares three readings of SLW loss: the Joule-literal default, the erratum's "no loss", and a charge-relaxation-only hypothesis. For a path through air all three give the same prediction, so an S21 in air cannot decide between them. They differ only in a conductive medium such as seawater (see that page). The Joule-literal reading remains the hub's default.

Button-by-button with the VNA6000-A

Where these steps come from. The NanoRFE VNA6000 User Manual, edition 2022/11 (30 pages). Every step below names its manual page. Menu names are written the way the manual writes them, in capitals, with an arrow between menu and item (for example CAL → RESET). Steps tagged IDEA are our advice, not the manual's. Your unit's firmware may be newer than the manual, so a menu name may differ; the "Not verified" list at the bottom says what the manual does not cover.

A. Set the frequency range (manual pp.11 to 12)

  1. Open the menu. Tap an empty spot on the touch screen (not on a marker), or press the ENTER button (p.7). You see: a menu list with DISPLAY, MARKER, STIMULUS, CAL, RECALL, CONFIG and CLOSE.
  2. Tap STIMULUS → START, type the number on the on-screen keypad, then tap the unit key (for example M for MHz). The unit key multiplies your number and ends the entry (p.8). Then STIMULUS → STOP the same way.
  3. Or use the middle and width instead: STIMULUS → CENTER and STIMULUS → SPAN (p.12).
  4. Suggested windows IDEA: Experiment A: START 1100 MHz, STOP 1500 MHz. Experiment B: START 400 MHz, STOP 470 MHz.
  5. Pause the sweep with STIMULUS → PAUSE SWEEP if you want a frozen picture (p.12). Turn it off again to keep measuring.

B. Calibrate (manual pp.13 to 14)

Always set the frequency range first. If you change the range afterwards, the old calibration is only stretched to fit and the manual says it "may degrade accuracy", so calibrate again (p.13).

Choose where the reference plane goes. Calibrate at the VNA ports (p.13) if you will plug the part straight in. Calibrate at the end of your cable (p.14) if a test cable will go between the VNA and the part. For our work you will nearly always want the cable version: keep the same cable on for calibration and measurement.

  1. Clear any old calibration: CAL → RESET, then → CALIBRATE (p.14, step 1).
  2. Attach your SMA cable to port 1 (p.14, step 2). Skip this step if you are calibrating at the port itself.
  3. Put a LOAD on port 2 (p.14, step 3).
  4. OPEN: screw the OPEN standard onto the end of the port-1 cable and tap → OPEN. Wait until the menu item lights up (highlights).
  5. SHORT: swap in the SHORT standard, tap → SHORT, wait for the highlight.
  6. LOAD: swap in the second LOAD standard, tap → LOAD, wait for the highlight. The manual says you need not wait for the plot to redraw before tapping the next item: each tap does one full sweep with extra averaging, and the highlight tells you it is done (p.14).
  7. (Only for S21 / through measurements) THRU: connect a second cable to port 2 and join the two cable ends with the THRU standard, then tap → THRU (p.14, steps 7 and 8). Then take the THRU out.
  8. Tap → DONE. You see: "Cx" on the left side of the screen, meaning calibration is active (p.13).
  9. Save it: CAL → SAVE → SAVE 0 (p.13 and p.22). IDEA Use slot 0; the manual says "0 to 6" in one place (p.13) and gives a different count on p.22, so we do not rely on higher slots.
  10. Check it: CAL → CORRECTION shows whether the correction is on (p.22). IDEA With the open standard on the cable end, S11 should read close to 0 dB across the range; with the load on, S11 should be very low. If not, redo the steps.
  11. To restore later: CAL → RECALL → RECALL 0 (p.22). Remember it only fits the range that was in use when you saved it.

C. Measure S11 (reflection) (manual pp.15 to 17)

  1. Connect the part to the port-1 cable end. Leave port 2 on its LOAD.
  2. Pick the channel: DISPLAY → CHANNEL → CH0 REFLECT. This is S11 (p.16).
  3. Pick how to draw it: DISPLAY → FORMAT → LOGMAG for a dB plot, or SWR for a standing-wave plot. The list is LOGMAG, PHASE, DELAY, SMITH, SWR, POLAR, LINEAR, REAL, IMAG, RESISTANCE, REACTANCE (p.16).
  4. Several views at once: up to four traces: DISPLAY → TRACE → TRACE n. Tapping the active trace turns it off (p.15). IDEA Trace 0: S11 LOGMAG; trace 1: S11 SWR; trace 2: the saved no-ball control, if you have one.
  5. Read a number: MARKER → SELECT MARKER → MARKER n, then drag it along the trace (p.17). Under MARKER → OPERATIONS you can send the marker to START, STOP, CENTER or SPAN (p.17). IDEA Slide the marker to the lowest point of the dip and write down the frequency and the dB.
  6. Save a picture of the plot: the manual does not describe a screenshot function on the device (see the "Not verified" list), so photograph the screen with your phone and note the calibration slot and sweep range.

D. Measure S21 (through / loss) (manual pp.14, 16)

  1. Calibrate with the THRU (Section B, steps 7 and 8) so port 2 is included.
  2. Connect the part between the cable ends: port-1 cable, then the part (filter, cable, attenuator), then port-2 cable.
  3. Pick the channel: DISPLAY → CHANNEL → CH1 THROUGH. This is S21 (p.16).
  4. Format: DISPLAY → FORMAT → LOGMAG (p.16). Put a marker at your frequency to read the loss in dB.
  5. Check the reading is not noise. A trace that is flat and jagged near −90 dB or lower is the instrument's floor (the data sheet gives 95 dB of dynamic range for the A model, p.4). IDEA Do not read anything near the floor as a result.

E. Cable test in the time domain (manual pp.18 to 21)

The VNA turns its frequency sweep into a distance picture (the manual calls it simulated TDR, time-domain reflectometry). Longer measurable distance needs a lower stop frequency; sharper distance accuracy needs a wider span (p.18).

  1. Turn it on: DISPLAY → TRANSFORM → TRANSFORM ON (p.18; the manual's own text spells the menu "TRANSOFRM" in one place). When it is on, the item shows as white text on black and the plot shows time or distance instead of frequency.
  2. For the "lowpass" style the manual says the start frequency must be 50 kHz, the stop frequency is set by how far you want to see, and the format can be REAL (p.19). The bandpass mode instead takes LINEAR, LOGMAG or SWR (p.18). Recalibrate for the range you choose.
  3. Enter the velocity factor of the cable as a whole number, for example 67 and not 0.67 (p.21: "specify 67 for the VELOCITY FACTOR. Do not use the decimal point"). Set it under DISPLAY → TRANSFORM → VELOCITY FACTOR. Look up the value for your own cable; we do not give one here.
  4. Choose a window: MINIMUM, NORMAL or MAXIMUM (p.21). MINIMUM gives the sharpest distance resolution, MAXIMUM the greatest dynamic range, NORMAL is in the middle (p.21). Start with NORMAL. IDEA
  5. Read the plot: a bump at a distance shows a reflection there (a connector or a damaged spot).

F. Using the PC program (manual section 4, pp.23 to 29)

  1. Download "VNA View" from the maker's software page (Links tab). It runs on Linux, Windows 7 or newer, and Mac OS 10.11 or newer (p.23). Connect the USB cable.
  2. Pick the device in the Device menu; a continuous sweep starts at once (p.25). Windows may need the Cypress USB serial driver from the VNA View downloads (p.25).
  3. Sweep settings are under Device → Sweep parameters; the "nValues" field under "Show advanced settings" is the averaging: higher means less noise and a slower sweep (p.26).
  4. Calibration on the PC: use Clear and Clear Measurements, choose SOL for one port or SOLT for two, click Open, Short, Load (and Thru for SOLT) in turn as you connect each standard, then Apply (p.27). Changing the sweep range on the PC always clears the active calibration (p.27).
  5. Save or load a calibration: Calibration → Save as… / Load… (p.27).

G. Switch-off checklist

  1. Photograph the plot, then write down the calibration slot, start / stop, cable ID and the date.
  2. Disconnect the part, then the cable. Put the connector savers back and cap the ports.
  3. Charge the battery if the LEDs are low (p.6). Remember the VNA stays off during any real listen with the cage closed.

  • Where the IF bandwidth is set on the device (the data sheet lists the values; the manual's menu list does not say).
  • Marker search (find minimum / maximum) and smoothing / averaging menus on the device itself.
  • Saving screenshots or traces on the device; exporting from the device.
  • Exact mode names in the time-domain menu beyond those listed, and any menu changes in firmware newer than 2022/11.
