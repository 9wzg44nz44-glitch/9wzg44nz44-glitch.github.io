Experiments A & B · stub antenna inside the sphere · mounting concepts

Mounting ideas: putting the stub tip at the center of the A and B spheres

GROK BOT INITIAL IDEAS — SUBJECT TO BETTER IDEAS FROM ANYONE These are starting points, not final designs. Nothing here has been built or tested. Steve Morris and anyone else with shop or RF experience: please improve, replace or reject any of them. Better ideas win.

Dan wants a short stub antenna (the bare center wire at the end of a coax cable) to poke into each hollow aluminum sphere so that the tip sits exactly at the sphere’s geometric center, with the connector and the sleeve balun kept outside. This page lists ways to hold the stub there, for the tiny Experiment A ball and the roomier Experiment B ball.

CORRECTED 5 Oct 2026 Experiment A: the centre-tip idea does not work on the ¾″ ball. The RG-405U shield must not touch the sphere, and the centre conductor is soldered into a #76 pin hole so that the sphere is the driven element. See A1 and exp-1296.html#feed. The Experiment B section is unchanged.

The short version

  • Experiment A (¾″ ball, 1296 MHz): CORRECTED 5 Oct 2026 the centre conductor is soldered into a #76 (0.020″) pin hole 2.5 mm deep, with 1.5 mm of PTFE exposed and the shield cut end 3.0 mm off the ball. The shield never touches the ball (A1, exp-1296.html#feed).
  • Experiment B (2.50″ ball, 433.59 MHz): the ball already has a ¼″ hole. A plastic “top-hat” bushing in that hole can center the coax with no metalwork on the ball. A plastic guide rod that spans the inside of the ball can put the tip at the center automatically.
  • Experiment B (A no longer uses a centre tip): the tip depth, measured from the outside surface at the hole, is the sphere radius: 31.75 mm for B (9.525 mm would be the A value). The wall thickness decides how much bare wire is inside. The radius, not λ/10, sets the stub length inside the ball.
  • Materials: non-ferrous only. Nylon, PTFE (Teflon) or Delrin (acetal) plastics; brass or nylon screws; no steel in the RF path.
Words used on this page (tap to hide)
  • Stub: the short length of bare center conductor left when the outer conductor and dielectric of a coax cable are stripped back. It is the antenna. On this hub it is cut from RG-405/U semi-rigid coax (tools.html, sleeve-balun.html).
  • Sleeve balun (also called a bazooka choke): a close-fitting metal tube over the coax, soldered to the coax outer conductor at one end (the “cold” end) and open at the other end, which faces the stub tip. It is about λ/4 long and reduces current on the outside of the coax. How-to: setup.html#sleeve.
  • λ (lambda): free-space wavelength, c/f, where c is the speed of light and f the frequency.
  • Bulkhead: a connector made to mount through a hole in a panel or wall, held by a nut or thread. The hub’s is the Amphenol 901-9875 SMA-female bulkhead.
  • Standoff: a non-conductive spacer that holds a part at a fixed distance or position.
  • R, t: sphere outer radius (half the outside diameter) and wall thickness.
  • Geometric center: the point inside the ball that is R from every point on its outside surface.
  • Labels: FACT from a datasheet, the patent or another hub page (linked). IDEA an untested suggestion. ASSUMPTION something we assume and still need to check. CONFLICT two hub sources disagree.

How small can the hole be?

  • CORRECTED 5 Oct 2026 Experiment A: only the 0.51 mm centre conductor enters the wall, so the hole is a #76 (0.020″) or #75 (0.021″) pin hole. The larger holes below are kept for Experiment B and for reference.
  • FACT Only the PTFE dielectric and center wire go through the wall (the outer conductor stops at the outside surface): the hole must clear 1.68 mm. A #50 drill (0.070″, 1.78 mm) is the next common size up.
  • FACT The whole RG-405 goes through (outer conductor inside the wall): the hole must clear ≈2.2 mm. A #43 drill (0.089″, 2.26 mm) is the next common size up.
  • FACT The 901-9875 bulkhead goes through the wall: 6.5 mm clearance (hub). On the ¾″ ball that hole is about one-third of the ball’s diameter.
  • IDEA Smaller holes keep the shell closer to a closed sphere and leave more metal around the hole. Try the drill on a spare ball first; the KB-1820 comes in a 10-pack (setup#sphere-a).

Before any design: measure the wall and plan the depth

  1. IDEA With the hole drilled (A) or using the stock hole (B), and nothing installed, put a caliper depth rod through the hole until it touches the far inside wall. Call the reading d.
  2. FACT (geometry) d = OD − t for the far wall. If the wall is uniform, t ≈ OD − d. Repeat at a few angles and on a spare ball.
  3. FACT (geometry) The tip belongs at depth R from the outside surface at the hole: 9.525 mm (A) or 31.75 mm (B). That holds whatever the wall thickness. (For Experiment A after the correction, use the wall reading to check that a 2.5 mm deep pin hole fits; see A1.)
  4. IDEA Put a fixed depth stop on the coax (a collar, a PTFE sleeve cut to length, or a paint mark read under magnification) so the insertion is repeatable.
  5. IDEA A flat caliper base rests on the hole rim, which is slightly below the top of the curve. From the sphere geometry that error is about 0.04 mm for a 1.8 mm hole in A, 0.16 mm for B’s ¼″ hole, but 0.57 mm for a 6.5 mm hole in A. For big holes, measure from a thin straightedge laid across the rim.

Experiment A — ¾″ KB-1820 at 1296 MHz

FACT No hole yet. The inside diameter is less than 19 mm, and the hub already says an interior locknut will not fit (“¾″ ID is too tight”, Sheet A). CORRECTED 5 Oct 2026 The Experiment A feed is now A1: shield off the ball, centre conductor soldered into a pin hole. A2 to A4 below are superseded. IDEA Every Experiment A concept holds the coax from the outside. Internal standoffs, internal 3D-printed cradles and internal nuts are judged not practical here: there is no way to get them in through a 2–6.5 mm hole, or to adjust them once they are inside.

A1 · Pin-hole feed (corrected 5 Oct 2026): centre conductor soldered into a #76 pin hole; shield kept off the ball

CORRECTED 5 Oct 2026 The earlier A1 text put the PTFE and wire through a #50 hole with the tip free at the center, and offered bonding the outer conductor to the ball. That was wrong for Experiment A: a shield bonded to the ball makes it a closed shield cap, and a free pin inside only excites a shorted cavity. The sphere is the driven element.

IDEA Derived for this hub, not from Monstein and Wesley. Build values (full table: exp-1296.html#feed):

  • Pin hole: #76 drill (0.020″, 0.51 mm), or #75 (0.021″, 0.53 mm) for a snug fit, 2.5 mm (0.10″) deep (2 to 3 mm).
  • Centre conductor soldered in the hole: aluminum solder with aluminum flux, or silver conductive epoxy. ASSUMPTION the ball is 3003 aluminum as listed; confirm on a spare ball.
  • PTFE exposed past the shield cut: 1.5 mm (0.06″).
  • Bare centre conductor past the PTFE end: 4.0 mm (0.16″), of which 2.5 mm (0.10″) is in the hole and 1.5 mm (0.06″) in air.
  • Shield cut end to sphere surface: 3.0 mm (0.12″). The shield never touches the ball.
  • If the wall is thinner than 2.5 mm, the hole goes through: insert the pin by the wall thickness only and log it.

Pros

Smallest possible hole; shell stays almost closed; no connector or extra hardware on the ball; the feed is about 0.024 λ long.

Cons

The one small joint carries all the mechanical load; ordinary tin-lead solder does not wet aluminum (FACT needs aluminum flux or conductive epoxy); the wall thickness is still unknown.

Sleeve: λ/4 = 57.8 mm (2.28″) in air, over the jacket and shield, far end soldered to the shield, open end toward the ball, level with the shield cut and never touching the ball. Pair it with A5 for strain relief.

A2 · External collar with a set screw: an adjustable depth, following the hub’s exterior-flange approach

CORRECTED 5 Oct 2026 Superseded for Experiment A. A metal collar bonds the shield to the ball and the tip sits inside it, which shorts the antenna. A nylon or Delrin collar could still serve as a strain relief that holds the shield 3.0 mm off the ball. Use A1 and exp-1296.html#feed. Kept for the record.

IDEA A small brass or aluminum collar (about 6–8 mm OD, a few mm long, with a saddle face that matches the ball’s curve) is bonded over a #43 (2.26 mm) hole. Its bore is a slip fit on the 0.086″ RG-405. A tiny brass or nylon set screw in the side of the collar clamps the coax once the tip depth is right. This is the same idea as the hub’s exterior flange/boss for the SMA (Sheet A, flange Ø ≈ 0.50″, 3–4 mm thick) scaled down to the coax.

Pros

Depth can be adjusted and re-checked; the collar adds grip length that the thin wall lacks; a non-metallic collar would keep the shield off the shell.

Cons

The collar must be bonded to aluminum (braze, aluminum solder or conductive epoxy); it adds a small metal lump outside the ball; a set screw can dent semi-rigid coax (use a nylon tip or a brass pad).
A3 · The hub’s SMA bulkhead path plus an internal extension wire

CORRECTED 5 Oct 2026 Superseded for Experiment A. The bulkhead body is bonded to the ball, so the shield is on the ball. Use A1 and exp-1296.html#feed. Kept for the record.

FACT The hub already has a shop plan for the ¾″ ball: an exterior flange or boss, a 6.5 mm clearance hole, and the 901-9875 body bonded to the shell (Sheet A). IDEA For a stub at the center, solder a short straight wire to the bulkhead’s center contact so its tip ends R = 9.525 mm below the outside surface. The sleeve goes on the external cable.

Pros

Matches the existing Sheet A; the ball can be unplugged; the thread is held by the flange, not the thin wall.

Cons

The 6.5 mm hole is large for a 19 mm ball; the bulkhead’s rear body may take up much of the space inside (ASSUMPTION: check the 901-9875 drawing); the extension adds a solder joint inside that you cannot see.

Tip at center: measure from the flange face and subtract the flange thickness. Sleeve: on the feed cable, open end toward the connector.

A4 · Potted plastic guide tube

CORRECTED 5 Oct 2026 Superseded for Experiment A. It puts a free tip inside the ball instead of driving the sphere. Use A1 and exp-1296.html#feed. Kept for the record.

IDEA Push a short Delrin or nylon tube (OD a slip fit in a #43 or slightly larger hole, bore a slip fit on the RG-405 or on its 1.68 mm dielectric) a millimetre or so through the wall. Hold it square in a jig and pot it from the outside with a small epoxy fillet. The coax then slides in to its depth stop and gets a drop of adhesive or a nylon set screw.

Pros

Sets the angle as well as the depth; spreads the load; no metal bonding to aluminum.

Cons

Permanent; epoxy and plastic sit right at the feed; FACT PTFE is hard to glue unless etched, so Delrin or nylon is easier here.
A5 · External non-metallic saddle (combine with A1, A2 or A4)

IDEA A 3D-printed (PETG or nylon) or machined Delrin saddle clamps the ball on one side and the coax (or sleeve) on the other, on one straight axis, and screws to the non-metallic TX sled with nylon screws. The small joint at the ball then carries no load when the cable is handled.

Pros

Protects the tiny joint; keeps the coax on the radial axis so the tip stays on the center; easy to print.

Cons

More parts; plastic near the ball (keep it thin and away from the hole if possible).

Experiment B — 2.50″ KB-2016 at 433.59 MHz

FACT The ball has a stock ¼″ (0.250″) hole, the wall is ≈3.35 mm, and the hub says not to tap that hole: 0.250″ is the thread’s major diameter, not a tap drill (sphere#sma-install). IDEA There is room inside, but anything that goes inside must pass through a hole about 6.35 mm wide.

B1 · Top-hat plastic bushing in the stock ¼″ hole: no metalwork on the ball

IDEA Turn a PTFE, Delrin or nylon bushing with a shank that is a snug fit in the 0.250″ hole (≈ wall length, 3.35 mm) and a flange outside. The center bore is a slip fit on the RG-405 (≈2.2 mm) or on its dielectric (1.68 mm). Hold it with a thin nylon nut or a split ring on the inside if you can reach it, or a small epoxy bead outside. A nylon set screw in the flange locks the coax at depth R = 31.75 mm.

Pros

Uses the hole as is; reversible; centers the coax on the hole axis; cheap.

Cons

The coax outer conductor is insulated from the shell unless you add a bond, which differs from the hub’s “outer as shield” default; PTFE creeps under set-screw pressure (Delrin is stiffer); the stock hole must point at the center (ASSUMPTION: check that the hole is radial).
B2 · Full-diameter plastic guide rod: the tip lands on the center by construction

IDEA Measure the inside diameter through the hole (method above: ID ≈ 63.5 − 2 × 3.35 ≈ 56.8 mm if the wall is uniform). Make a Delrin or PTFE rod just under 6.3 mm OD, as long as the measured ID, with a 0.55 mm blind bore drilled along its axis from one end to exactly half its length. Slide it in until it touches the far wall. The bare stub then goes into the bore, and the tip bottoms out at the midpoint of the rod, which is the geometric center. Hold the rod’s outer end with B1’s bushing.

Pros

Self-locating and easy to check: if the rod touches the far wall, the tip is at the center; the stub is supported along its length and cannot bend.

Cons

Puts a plastic rod across the whole diameter, so the stub is embedded in dielectric (IDEA: compare S11 with and without it on the VNA6000); a long 0.55 mm bore is hard to drill straight; the rod is hard to remove.
B3 · The hub’s SMA bulkhead path plus an internal extension wire

FACT Hub plan for B: keep or ream the stock hole to 6.5 mm clearance and clamp the 901-9875 with a locknut and washer through the wall. Do not tap (Sheet B). There is room for the inside nut on this ball. IDEA Solder a straight wire to the bulkhead’s center contact so its tip ends 31.75 mm below the outside surface.

Pros

Matches Sheet B; the ball can be unplugged; the thread is held by a nut, not the wall.

Cons

Reaming changes the stock hole; the extension joint is hidden inside; a long bare extension can sag unless supported (combine with B2’s rod).
B4 · External clamp collar (B-sized version of A2)

IDEA A brass collar with a curved saddle face is bonded over the stock hole, with a bore for the RG-405 and a brass or nylon set screw. Alternatively, a split brass clamp ring around a PTFE sleeve on the coax, screwed to an external saddle.

Pros

Adjustable depth; bonds the outer conductor to the shell if you want that; no work inside the ball.

Cons

Bonding brass to aluminum (use conductive epoxy or a mechanical clamp); the collar must be square to the hole axis.
B5 · Potted Delrin tube and B6 · machined boss (heavier options)

IDEA B5: as in A4, a Delrin or nylon tube in the ¼″ hole, potted from outside. B6: a machined aluminum boss bonded over the hole, thick enough for ≥2–3 full thread pitches (FACT hub rule; 1/4-36 pitch = 1/36″ ≈ 0.71 mm, so ≥1.4–2.1 mm of boss), then tapped 1/4-36 UNEF 2B in the boss only. FACT Monstein & Wesley’s Fig. 1 ball used a machined 18 mm shaft and 34 mm base at the feed (sphere.html). A boss is the catalog-ball version of that.

Pros

Strong and permanent; a boss gives a real thread.

Cons

Needs a machine shop; permanent; more metal at the feed.

IDEA Internal 3D-printed cradle for B: a full cradle cannot pass through the ¼″ hole, so it would mean cutting and re-joining the ball. Not recommended; B2’s rod is the “internal support” that fits through the hole.

Checking that the tip really is at the center

  • IDEA Depth stop plus measurement: the stop is set to R from the tip (9.525 mm A, 31.75 mm B), measured under magnification with calipers before assembly.
  • IDEA Sacrificial ball (A): the KB-1820 is sold in a 10-pack. Drill one exactly like the real ones, then saw it in half to see where the tip ends up. It also serves as a drilling practice piece.
  • IDEA Printed see-through mockup (B): print a clear 63.5 mm half-shell with a ¼″ hole in the same place, and set the depth stop against it before touching the real ball (only two KB-2016 were bought, sphere.html).
  • IDEA X-ray: a dental or industrial X-ray shows the copper-clad wire against the thin aluminum shell; a quick way to confirm on the finished part.
  • IDEA Borescope or phone light (B only, before the coax goes in): a small inspection camera through the ¼″ hole can check for swarf and burrs inside. It is not precise enough to locate the tip.
  • IDEA S11 repeatability: log S11 for each assembly on the VNA6000-A. It does not tell you where the tip is, but a TX/RX pair that differs a lot suggests the two builds are not the same.

Practical build notes

  • IDEA Holding a 19 mm ball for drilling: a V-block or soft (aluminum or wood) vise jaws with a matching cup, so the drill axis passes through the center and the thin wall is not crushed. Line up the drill with the V-block centerline.
  • IDEA Starting the hole: a light spot with a sharp center drill or a very light center punch (a heavy punch can dent a thin stamped wall). Then a small pilot, then the final size or a step drill. Low speed and light feed.
  • IDEA Deburr inside and out; no swarf left inside. Blow out, rinse with isopropyl alcohol, shake and listen: a rattle means a chip is still inside. Drilling with the hole facing down helps chips fall out.
  • FACT Thread engagement: the hub says do not tap a thin ball wall; tap 1/4-36 only in a boss at least 2–3 pitches (≈1.4–2.1 mm) thick (setup#sphere-a, shop drawing). ASSUMPTION The KB-1820 wall is probably thinner than that; measure it. If it is, use a nut (only B has room), a boss (A2, A3, B6) or epoxy (A4, B5).
  • FACT Connector and sleeve stay outside the ball in every concept here, as Dan asked.
  • FACT Non-ferrous only: 3003 aluminum, brass, copper, PTFE, Delrin and nylon are all non-ferrous. No steel screws, washers or clamps in the RF path.

How each concept fits the sleeve balun

FACT Hub sleeve rule (Keyport-style): a close-fitting tube over the RG-405 jacket, soldered to the outer conductor at the cold end (away from the tip), open end toward the stub tip; λ/4 in air = 57.8 mm (A) or 172.9 mm (B); trim shorter if the gap has tape or PTFE (sleeve-balun.html, setup#sleeve). With the stub inside the ball, the sleeve’s open end faces the ball.

CORRECTED 5 Oct 2026 Experiment A: the sleeve’s open end is level with the shield cut, about 3.0 mm from the ball, and never touches it (exp-1296.html#feed).

IDEA Open questions for Dan, Steve or anyone else: How big a gap should there be between the sleeve’s open end and the ball surface? Should the sleeve touch the ball, or be kept off it with a thin PTFE washer? For Experiment A it is settled: the coax outer conductor must not touch the ball (A1). For Experiment B, should the outer conductor be bonded to the shell (the hub’s “outer as shield” default, from Monstein and Wesley Fig. 1) or insulated from it? These choices change the electrical setup, so pick one, log it, and keep the TX and RX identical.

What the references actually say

US 12,525,711 B1 (Paulus & Gentile, US Navy / NUWC Keyport, issued 13 Jan 2026): local PDF
  • FACT Col. 6, lines 27–47 (FIGS. 7A–7B): the monopole antenna “may further comprise a spherical transmitter 80 enclosing the antenna portion 21 and the shielded portion 23 of the center conductor 20.” The sphere “may be made of conductive materials such as various metallic elements and alloys that may include copper,” “may be substantially solid, and molded or adapted to connect to the balun 60,” “may comprise an antenna cavity 81,” and “may be connected to a cable jacket 26.” The sphere “may have a radius 80′, and a balun length 60″ equivalent to the radius 80′,” and “the radius 80′ may be equivalent to a fourth of a wavelength.” “The spherical transmitter 80 may comprise the antenna tube 29.”
  • FACT Claims 10–17 repeat this: the spherical transmitter encloses the antenna portion and the shielded portion (claim 10), a metallic sphere (11), substantially solid and adapted to connect to the balun (12), an antenna cavity (13), connected to the cable jacket (14), balun length equal to the sphere radius (16), radius equal to λ/4 (17).
  • FACT The balun attaches to the shield: “a balun 60 may be connected to the shield 40 at a contact location 61 on the shield 40 … located on an exterior surface 62 of the shield,” with a closed end at the contact and an open end facing the tip (claims 1 and 6).
  • FACT In FIG. 7A the balun (60), shield (40) and antenna tube (29) are drawn inside the sphere’s cavity (81), and the cable jacket (26) enters through the sphere wall. FIG. 7B shows a solid, hatched sphere with the balun embedded in it. The patent says its drawings are “not necessarily to scale.”
  • DIFFERENCE Dan’s layout is not the patent’s FIG. 7. The patent puts the balun inside a sphere whose radius is λ/4, which would be 57.8 mm at 1296 MHz or 172.9 mm at 433.59 MHz. Our balls have radii of 9.525 mm and 31.75 mm, so the sleeve cannot fit inside and stays outside. IDEA In FIG. 7A the radius line 80′ appears to start at the end of the shield, with the antenna tube continuing past it. The patent does not say the tip is at the center; putting the tip at the center is Dan’s choice.
  • FACT The patent gives no mounting hardware for the sphere: no fasteners, potting or standoffs. Nothing on this page comes from the patent except the idea that a sphere encloses the antenna and the balun attaches to the shield.
Tomilin, Lukin & Gulkov 2021 (Russian marine channel, ball and rod): library card · kit gap list

FACT (hub library card) A 60 mm copper sphere is joined by a metal rod to the center of a Tesla flat spiral at 27.4 MHz, and the whole antenna is insulated from seawater by a dielectric layer. The rod meets the sphere from the outside; it is not a stub inside the sphere. It is a useful example of holding a sphere on a rod, not a template for a stub at the center. The physics claims are contested (see the card).
