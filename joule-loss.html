Joule loss and power balance

Can we measure how much of the transmitted wave is lost as Joule heating, using the polarising-rod attenuator at each frequency? Short answer: we can measure the insertion loss and its dependence on rod angle, material and frequency very well. We cannot, with a VNA and a spectrum analyzer alone, separate rod heating from rod scattering. This page says exactly what is and is not measurable, gives a test that can separate the main loss readings, and offers calculators.

Tests of hypotheses, not results. Mainstream electrodynamics treats free-space radio as transverse (TEM). A longitudinal or scalar component is a hypothesis we are testing, and nothing on these pages claims that scalar or longitudinal waves have been detected. 1. Three things people mean by "Joule loss"

  1. Insertion loss of the rod array: how many dB the received signal drops when the rods are in. measurable directly from S21 or from the spectrum analyzer.
  2. Power actually turned into heat in the rods. not directly measurable here. At milliwatt drive the heating is of order micro-kelvin (rod-thermal), far below any thermometer we have. It has to be inferred.
  3. The Joule term σE∥² in the theory. In Monstein and Wesley (2002) it is the whole explanation of the rod curve (Eq. 7 to 9). Hively and Loebl's later papers disagree with each other about whether an SLW has resistive loss at all (joule-loss-positions). model

The experiment can measure (1) and use materials and frequencies to test which version of (3) is consistent. It cannot, on its own, give (2).

3. The rod attenuator model

fact (as published) Monstein and Wesley, p. 516 to 517: only the E component along a wire, E0 cos φ, produces Ohmic loss. Eq. (7): δP = -σA E0² cos²φ δL. Eq. (8): δ⟨S⟩ = -(σ/c) cos²φ ⟨S⟩ δL. Eq. (9): T = exp[-κ cos²φ], with κ "a constant proportional to the length L of the wire". Their fit used κ = 4.4 for brass at 433.59 MHz; κ was fitted, not computed from σ.

T(φ) = T0 exp[-κ cos²(φ - φ0)] ⇔ level(dB) = L0 - 4.343 κ cos²(φ - φ0)

For κ = 4.4 the loss with the rods aligned (φ = φ0) is 19.1 dB (T = 0.012). The calculator below fits this form by least squares (the angle φ0 is scanned, L0 and κ are linear) and reports a standard error.

fact A wire array attenuates the field component along its wires for an ordinary transverse wave too (it is a wire-grid polariser). So a good fit to this form does not distinguish an SLW from a TEM wave. What can be compared is how κ behaves.

The test. Run the identical scan with brass and then W1 rods (same plates, same positions). If κ(W1)/κ(brass) is near 0.36 or 0.27 with a small error, the literal Joule reading is favoured and scattering is not the whole story. If it is near 1, the literal reading is disfavoured. If it matches neither, something else (fixture repeatability, W1 magnetism, rod length error) is at work. Then check the band scaling with the same material. proposal Decide the acceptance thresholds before taking data.

5. Ordinary-physics control: skin depth and rod resistance

fact (transverse-EM reasoning, not applicable under the SLW hypothesis) The skin depth is δ = 1/√(π f μ0μr σ) and the surface resistance is Rs = 1/(σδ). Conductivities and sources:

  • Brass C36000: 26% IACS, resistivity 6.63 µΩ·cm, σ = 1.508 × 107 S/m (Aviva Metals data sheet; MakeItFrom C36000).
  • W1 annealed: resistivity 0.185 µΩ·m (EngMats C80W1; a steel-mill datasheet gives 0.20 Ω·mm²/m = 5.0 × 106 S/m; the hub's rod-thermal page uses 5.0 × 106 from MetalZenith 0.18 to 0.22 µΩ·m), σ = 5.4 × 106 S/m used here.
  • W1 hardened: 7.1% IACS (MakeItFrom, hardened W1), σ = 4.1 × 106 S/m. The hub's rod-thermal page uses 3.3 × 106 S/m (MatWeb, 30 µΩ·cm). The spread is real; measure your rods' resistance if it matters.
  • Copper: 5.8 × 107 S/m (IACS definition).
  • W1 relative permeability: not sourced. W1 is a plain carbon steel and ferromagnetic, but we found no value at GHz. The last row uses μr = 100 purely as an assumption to show the sensitivity; the effective permeability of steel at hundreds of MHz is usually far lower than the low-frequency value, and it needs measuring.

All depths are a few micrometres, far below the 1.5875 mm rod radius, so a TEM wave sees every one of these rods as a good conductor and mostly reflects or scatters it. The calculator estimates, for a resonant half-wave rod, the fraction of intercepted power that becomes heat: about 0.1 to 0.5% for brass and W1 with μr = 1, up to a few percent if W1 behaved with μr = 100. If the measured insertion loss is 10 to 20 dB while a TEM estimate of heating is well under 1% of intercepted power, nearly all of the TEM loss is scattering. That is why S21 alone cannot give Joule heating.

6. Power balance, and the limit of what S-parameters can tell you

For power incident from the TX port, conservation gives:

Pin = Preflected + Preceived + Pabsorbed in rods + Pscattered elsewhere + Pother losses

The VNA gives Preflected = |S11|²Pin and Preceived = |S21|²Pin. Two cases:

  • A true two-port fixture (everything the RX does not get goes into the fixture): dissipated fraction = 1 - |S11|² - |S21|². Valid if nothing radiates away. It does not hold for two antennas in open air.
  • Two antennas in air (our case): use the difference between rods in and rods out with an identical setup. The fraction of received power the rods remove is 1 - Tin/Tout. The part of that which shows up as extra input reflection is small unless the rods detune the TX. The rest is absorbed or scattered away from the RX, and S-parameters cannot split those.

What can split them.

  1. Material swap (section 4): scattering by a good conductor is nearly the same for brass and W1, absorption is not (if the absorption model holds). A change in loss between materials is an absorption difference.
  2. Closed-box power balance proposal: put the rod array inside a small shielded enclosure fed by two ports through the walls, measure S11 and S21 with and without rods. Then 1 - |S11|² - |S21|² is wall plus rod dissipation, and the empty-box value removes the walls. This does not reproduce free-space propagation, so it tests the rods as absorbers, not the free-space claim.
  3. Anechoic or reverberation chamber: integrates all scattered power. Not available to us.
  4. Calorimetry: needs watts. Only the oven is that strong (see section 8, and never with rods inside).

8. The microwave oven: an equivalent power balance, done safely

Safety first. Never connect the VNA, a TinySA or any probe to the oven's antenna, waveguide or cavity. Never put rods, spheres or any metal inside a running oven. Never run it empty. Receive only, from outside, through an attenuator. The oven page has the full safety list.

You cannot do the VNA two-port balance on an oven, because it is its own source. The equivalent pieces are:

  1. What the magnetron delivers: water calorimetry in the cavity, P = m c ΔT / t, with m in kg, c = 4186 J/(kg·K). A 1 kg load heated 10 K in 60 s corresponds to about 700 W (calculator E). Water-load calorimetry is the usual way to estimate oven output; a formal standard procedure exists (IEC 60705), so check its exact method before quoting any figure.
  2. What leaks to the receiver: a receive-only 3/4 in sphere into a spectrum analyzer, through a fixed attenuator, at a fixed geometry. Record the level in dBm; convert to mW. Without a calibrated antenna this is a relative number. Use channel power across 2.40 to 2.50 GHz, not a single bin, because the magnetron frequency wanders.
  3. What the rods do: the same rod scan as the other bands, now on the leaked signal. Fit κ and compare with the sphere-transmitter κ at 2.45 GHz and with the brass versus W1 ratio.

Limit: this never gives the leaked fraction of the magnetron output, only changes in a relative level. Oven output varies with power setting (duty cycling), load and line voltage, so run at full power with a fixed load and repeat.

9. Limits and how this could fail

  • A good fit to T = exp(-κ cos²φ) is expected from ordinary physics. It is not a detection.
  • If brass and W1 give the same κ and κ does not scale as 1/f, the literal Joule reading is out. That is a useful result.
  • If the TX sphere rotated 90° moves the curve, the loss is tied to the transmitter's polarisation, as for an ordinary wave.
  • The VNA phase and amplitude drift with temperature and cable movement; repeat runs and keep raw data.
  • Near-field coupling, plate effects and the acrylic's dielectric loss all show up in S21. The plates-only baseline removes some of them.
  • Everything labelled "proposal" (closed-box balance, thresholds, stub lengths at 2.45 GHz) is untested.
