Ranging a Scalar-Longitudinal Transmitter

Goal: estimate how far away a scalar-longitudinal-wave (SLW) transmitter is, using what a receiver can measure as it moves. We build up in three layers: fixed checkpoints from ground-reflection nulls, a two-detector trick to fill in between them, and a more speculative two-detector ratio.

Educational only. Mainstream classical electrodynamics treats free-space radio as transverse (TEM); scalar-longitudinal waves are disputed. Sections 1 and 2 use plain two-path geometry that works for any wave. Section 3 depends on unverified extended-electrodynamics assumptions.

1 · Null checkpoints FACT

FACT The ground acts like a mirror. The receiver gets the direct wave from the transmitter plus a reflected wave that seems to come from an “image” transmitter below the ground. The reflected path is a little longer. Wherever that extra length is an odd number of half-wavelengths, the two waves partly cancel and the signal dips. These dips (nulls) sit at fixed distances that depend only on the wavelength and the two antenna heights. The result holds for any kind of wave, so it does not depend on any SLW theory.

The published case: Monstein & Wesley (2002)

  • FACT Frequency 433.59 MHz, so λ = c/f = 0.6914 m. Transmitter ball 4.0 m above ground; receiver ball 4.4 m above ground.
  • FACT To agree with their observations, M&W put the effective reflecting plane λ/2 = 34.6 cm below the ground surface (the ground conducts poorly, about 10⁻³ S/m). That gives effective heights y = 4.346 m and y′ = 4.746 m. ASSUMPTION The same λ/2 offset applies to your ground; it depends on soil conductivity.
  • FACT Distances: direct path R = √(x² + (y′ − y)²); image path R′ = √(x² + (y′ + y)²), where x is the horizontal distance.
  • FACT Their large-distance signal (eq. 15):
    S(x) ≈ (ωk / 2x²) · [A² + B² + 2AB·cos k(R′ − R)],   k = 2π/λ
    Here A and B are the direct and image amplitudes. From their data B²/A² = 0.482, so B/A = 0.694.
  • FACT Minima occur when R′ − R = (2n + 1)·λ/2, for n = 0, 1, 2, … (eq. 17). Larger n gives a null closer to the transmitter.
  • FACT M&W’s Table I predicts minima at 119, 39, 23 and 16 m (n = 0…3). They report that their observations agree at 39 m and 23 m. For the other two they say the minima “probably exist” but noise and roughly ±5 m of GPS error made them indefinite.
  • FACT Our own calculation from their effective heights gives 119.2, 39.3, 23.0 and 15.8 m (plus 11.7 m and closer). Each dip reaches (1 − B/A)², which is 10.3 dB below the direct-wave-only level. The calculator below reproduces these numbers.
  • FACT Measured fall-off: in the range 20–100 m, M&W’s data fit (1/x²)·[1 + 0.945·cos k(R′ − R)]. Beyond about 100 m the signal dropped faster than 1/x²: an extra exponential loss of 1/e per 145 m, which they attribute to ground losses.

Which null am I on?

Every null looks alike, so a single dip does not tell you which one it is. Two practical ways to find out:

  1. FACT Count from a known position. Start close to the transmitter at a distance you have measured, then walk or drive outward and count the dips. Going outward, n decreases by 1 at each dip; the last dip (n = 0) is the farthest.
  2. FACT Switch frequency. Null positions scale with frequency: for far nulls, x grows roughly in proportion to f. Change f by a known amount (for example +10%) and see which dip moves by the amount the calculator predicts for each n. The matching prediction identifies n. At 1.1 × 433.59 MHz the 39.3 m null moves to about 42.7 m and the 119.2 m null to about 129.3 m.

2 · Wavefront-curvature fill-in (between nulls)

Nulls act as mile-markers, but you are usually somewhere between two of them. To fill the gap, place two identical SLW detectors a known distance d apart on the line pointing at the transmitter. The nearer detector sits at r₁ and the farther one at r₂ = r₁ + d. Because the wave spreads out, the nearer detector reads slightly stronger.

  • FACT M&W measured power falling as 1/x² (amplitude ∝ 1/x) from 20 to 100 m, with extra loss beyond 100 m.
  • ASSUMPTION Amplitude falls as 1/r at your site over the baseline.
  • FACT (arithmetic, given that assumption) A₁/A₂ = r₂/r₁ = (r₁ + d)/r₁, so
    r₁ = d / (A₁/A₂ − 1)
    No Josephson junction or exotic detector is needed, just two matched SLW detectors.
  • FACT Divide out the ground ripple first. Each reading includes the multipath factor F from Section 1, and F differs between the two positions. Divide each amplitude by √F at its own position before taking the ratio. This needs B/A to have been measured, for example from null depth.
  • FACT Sensitivity is the hard part. At 80 m with d = 2 m the amplitude ratio is only 1.025 (0.21 dB). An error of just ±0.02 dB moves the answer to about 73–88 m. You need detectors and electronics stable to about 0.02 dB. A longer baseline helps: the ratio grows roughly in proportion to d.
  • FACT If M&W’s extra loss (1/e per 145 m in power) is present and not removed, it adds about 0.06 dB across a 2 m baseline. That makes the 80 m example read about 62 m. ASSUMPTION M&W saw this extra loss beyond 100 m; whether it also acts at shorter range at your site is unknown.

A ratio in dB is the same number whether you read power or amplitude, because dB = 20·log₁₀(amplitude ratio) = 10·log₁₀(power ratio).

3 · Two-detector ratio HYPOTHESIS

This idea uses two different detectors: an SLW power detector and a Josephson-junction (JJ) phase detector. Both see the same transmitter current I₀. If their readings depend on I₀ in different ways, dividing one by the other could cancel the unknown transmitter strength. This section works through when that also leaves range information and when it does not.

  • HYPOTHESIS SLW detector power: PL = CL·I₀²·r−p·F(r), where F is the Section 1 ground factor and p is the power fall-off exponent.
  • HYPOTHESIS JJ phase: φ = CJ·I₀·r−1·G(r). This assumes the junction responds linearly to the vector potential A, per Hively & Loebl’s extended electrodynamics as relayed by Dan; it has not yet been checked against the paper’s equation numbers. ASSUMPTION G = 1 (no ground-reflection effect in the JJ channel).
  • FACT (algebra, given those models) PL/φ² = (CL/CJ²)·r2−p·F(r). The transmitter current I₀ cancels.
  • FACT (algebra) If p = 2 (both channels effectively 1/r in amplitude), range cancels too. Any combination PLm·φn carries I₀ and r with the same exponent (2m + n), so removing I₀ always removes r.
  • HYPOTHESIS The method therefore works only if the SLW field falls as 1/r² (power p = 4). You also need one calibration shot at a known distance rcal. Then:
    r = rcal · √[ (φ²/PL) ÷ (φcal²/Pcal) · F(r)/F(rcal) ]
    Because F(r) depends on the unknown r, the equation has to be solved numerically.

Worked example (verified in JavaScript and Python)

  • ASSUMPTION p = 4, G = 1, and M&W geometry and B/A (Section 1 defaults). Calibration at rcal = 20 m; the true target is at 80 m.
  • FACT F(20 m) = 2.246 (R′ − R = 1.966 m); F(80 m) = 1.424 (R′ − R = 0.514 m).
  • FACT PL ratio = (20/80)⁴ × 1.424/2.246 = 0.00248, i.e. −26.1 dB. That splits into −24.1 dB from distance and −2.0 dB from the ground path. φ ratio = 20/80 = 0.250.
  • FACT Quantity (φ ratio)² ÷ PL ratio = 0.0625 / 0.00248 = 25.24.
  • FACT Without the F correction: r = 20·√25.24 = 100.5 m, which is 26% too far. With F(80)/F(20) = 0.634: r = 80.0 m.
  • FACT Watch out: more than one answer. Because F ripples, the equation has several solutions between 10 and 300 m. For this example they are 22.7, 23.3, 37.0, 42.4 and 80.0 m. Simple repeated substitution starting from F ratio = 1 jumps around (100.5 → 38.0 → 26.3 → 91.3 → 55.3 …) and does not settle reliably. The calculator therefore scans the whole range and lists every solution.
  • FACT Picking the right answer: use the null bracket from Section 1. Here both 42.4 and 80.0 m lie between the 39.3 m and 119.2 m nulls, so the bracket alone is not enough; add the Section 2 curvature estimate. ASSUMPTION A repeat at a second frequency also works if the model holds at both frequencies: the true distance stays put while the false solutions move.

4 · Caveats, unknowns and falsifiers

What does not depend on SLW theory

  • FACT Null positions depend only on wavelength and the two (effective) heights.
  • FACT The F correction is plain geometry once B/A has been measured at your site.

What depends on extended electrodynamics (EED)

  • HYPOTHESIS A pure scalar wave exists with E = 0 and B = 0.
  • HYPOTHESIS Its 1/r fall-off, which Section 2 and the JJ channel in Section 3 rely on.
  • HYPOTHESIS A linear Josephson-junction response to it.
  • HYPOTHESIS The power exponent p (range information needs p = 4).

Unknowns

  • ASSUMPTION G = 1: the reflection coefficient for the JJ channel is unknown.
  • ASSUMPTION The ratio of source strengths for SW (scalar wave) and SLW is fixed; nobody has checked that it is.

Falsifiers: results that would sink the idea

  • (a) PL/φ² stays flat as distance changes. That means p = 2, so the ratio carries no range information.
  • (b) The JJ nulls fall at different distances from the SLW nulls. Then the two detectors are not seeing the same wave.
  • (c) The JJ reading does not respond when the transmitter is keyed on and off, or does not fall as 1/r.
  • (d) Either reading survives the controls: transmitter off, a dummy load in place of the antenna, and the detector sealed in a Faraday cage. FACT A sealed cage strips ordinary TEM radio. HYPOTHESIS SLW passes through the cage wall.

FACT Practical point: Josephson junctions need cryogenic cooling and magnetic shielding. This is not a backyard detector.
