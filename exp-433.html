Round 1 · 433.59 MHz · sphere transmitter, sphere receiver, polarising-rod attenuator

Experiment B · 433.59 MHz

The Monstein and Wesley (2002) replication: two aluminium balls, one transmitting and one receiving, with a 3 x 3 array of half-wave brass rods turned through φ between them. Their published curve is T = exp(-κ cos²φ) with a fitted κ = 4.4 for brass. No balun of any kind on this bench. Round 1 repeats it with brass and then W1 tool steel (annealed) rods, each turned through a full 360°, first on the open bench with no Faraday cage and then with the big spheres inside a closed Faraday cage. It also adds VNA S21 baselines, an automatic range estimate and a Joule-loss analysis.

Tests of hypotheses, not results. Mainstream electrodynamics treats free-space radio as transverse (TEM). A longitudinal or scalar component is a hypothesis we are testing, and nothing on these pages claims that scalar or longitudinal waves have been detected. Values marked proposal are our suggestions and are not measured or sourced. Draft v0.1, 4 Oct 2026, written by Grok Bot for Dan Britton.

2. Run order

Two runs, from Dan's 5 Oct 2026 email to Steve ("Big Sphere Experiment - no Faraday Cage Required to replicate Monstein & Wesley 2002").
  • Scan change (Dr. Hively's requested change, per Dan's 5 Oct 2026 email to Steve). Swap the brass rods for W1 tool steel (annealed) and turn the lazy Susan a full 360°. Both rod sets get the same full scan: φ = 0 to 360° in 10° steps (37 readings; the 360° reading is the return-to-start check). Monstein and Wesley's Fig. 3 covers φ (the angle between the wire direction and the propagation direction) from -90° to +90°, a 180° span, with the fit T = exp(-4.4 cos²φ). Our Exp B extends this to the full 0 to 360°, per Dr. Hively's request.
  • Run 1, primary: open bench, no Faraday cage. Per Dan's email, this data collection does not need the cage.
  • Run 2, planned: the same scans with the big spheres inside a closed Faraday cage. Its purpose is to answer the objection "you're not using a theoretical sphere, you drilled a hole in it, thus TEM waves are possible." proposal Cage layout still to be agreed: each sphere, with its feed and SMA bulkhead, in its own closed enclosure (the Experiment A layout) addresses the objection directly. A single cage around both spheres would still allow an ordinary TEM path between them inside the cage. Optional further step: the caged rod fixture configuration (section 6).
  • Sphere option (optional). If the drilled-hole approach needs modification, the machine shop can machine a sphere with the flange built in as one part, as Monstein and Wesley did (Fig. 1). Decision item on the To-Do page.
  1. Inspect the rods (length, diameter, magnet and mass test) on the To-Do page. Do not start until the bags are labelled brass or W1.
  2. Calibrate the VNA in slot 0 (380 to 490 MHz); full SOLT with the TX and RX cables, then check that a THRU reads 0 dB (vna-howto.html).
  3. Noise floor: terminate both ports and record S21 with the settings you will use. Anything within 10 dB of that floor is not usable.
  4. S11 of each sphere at the SMA reference plane. Record the lowest dip and the value at 433.59 MHz. Do not tune to chase a number; log it and compare after any change.
  5. S21 baseline with no rods at a tape-measured reference range r0 (sphere centre to sphere centre). The hub's Experiment B guidance is a baseline of 2 to 3 λ or more (about 1.4 to 2.1 m and up), with the rod cube at least λ/(2π) = 110 mm from either ball (monstein.html#ranging-b). Candidate ranges: 2λ = 1.38 m, 3λ = 2.07 m, 4λ = 2.77 m, 6λ = 4.15 m, 8λ = 5.53 m (proposal). Save frequency, dB and phase as CSV.
  6. Plates only (acrylic plates and bases, no rods): repeat the S21 baseline. This isolates the plate.
  7. Range check: paste the S21 sweep into the range finder and compare its estimate with the tape.
  8. Run 1 (open, no cage), brass rods, full angle scan: rotate the H array through φ = 0 to 360° in 10° steps (37 readings; the 360° reading is the return-to-start check), logging S21 magnitude at the carrier each time. The 0° and 360° readings should agree; if they do not, the fixture moved or the bench drifted. The V array stays fixed. Run this first with the fixture MIDWAY; the three-station version is in section 5.
  9. Settle after each step: after every 10° turn, hands off and wait about 3 s (or until the live trace is flat) before reading, so the rods stop ringing. Log it as settle_s. Estimates and reasons: rod-jig page, section 7.
  10. Fit the scan to T = T0 exp(-κ cos²(φ - φ0)) on the Joule-loss page. Keep κ, φ0, T0 and the residual.
  11. Run 1 (open), W1 tool steel (annealed) rods: the same full 0 to 360° scan with the same plates, positions, range and VNA settings (Dr. Hively's requested change, per Dan's 5 Oct 2026 email to Steve). Fit again and compare κ with the brass value.
  12. Independent cross-check with a TinySA Ultra as receiver (peak hold) for one brass and one W1 scan.
  13. Dipole Detector DD-433 in place of the receiving sphere: rotate it through 360°, fit the figure-eight on the range-finder page.
  14. Repeat on a second day. Save every raw file; edit nothing.
  15. Run 2 (planned), closed Faraday cage: repeat the brass and W1 full 360° scans with the big spheres inside a closed Faraday cage, same range and settings, to answer the drilled-hole TEM objection. Record the cage layout, closed state and grounding in every row.

Ranges are centre to centre. Keep cables taped down after calibration; a moved cable is a phase error. Never exceed +10 dBm or 5 V DC on a VNA port.

5. Three-position fixture scan (proposal)

Added 4 Oct 2026. Analysis calculators: Joule-loss page, section 8. Checklist: To-Do group.

hypothesis Dan's early-attenuation idea. A rotating rod fixture placed close to the transmitter should give a more measurable change in the detected amplitude than one placed midway or near the receiver, because paths that would otherwise reflect off objects between TX and RX are attenuated before they can reflect. To keep the fixture out of the source's reactive near field, the closest point of approach of any rod during the rotation must stay at least λ/(2π) from the TX sphere centre: 110.0 mm at 433.59 MHz, 36.8 mm at 1296 MHz, 19.5 mm at 2450 MHz. This is a hypothesis test and a proposal. Nothing here is a measured result.

Where does the fixture go? The sources do not say. Monstein and Wesley (2002) report testing the wave by "intervening a cubic array of 9 half-wavelength wires" (abstract) and call it a polarizer-analyzer. They give no distance from either ball. Butterworth et al. (2013), describing that apparatus, write "Between Tx and Rx are positioned a pair of rotatable polarizer-analyzer arrays", which is where the word "between" comes from. Neither source fixes a position along the line, so this test treats the position as a variable to measure.

Pivot means the turntable axis. The V array (rods vertical, fixed) travels with the rotating array as one fixture; give it the same clearance and log the H to V spacing. If your turntable axis is not through the array centre, recompute with calculator G.

Protocol

  1. Fix the geometry. Choose r once (4λ above, or your own) and keep heights, cables, TX power, VNA settings and cage state fixed. Only the fixture moves, along the non-metallic rail.
  2. Mark the three pivot positions and hand-rotate the fixture through 360° at each near station with a tape: no rod point may come closer than 110.0 mm to the nearer sphere centre.
  3. Reference runs at every station: rods out, plates only (no rods), and an ambient baseline with the TX off.
  4. Angle scan at every station: φ = 0 to 360° in 10° steps (37 readings; the 360° reading is the return-to-start check), on the open bench (Run 1) and, when it is set up, in the closed cage (Run 2). At each angle log the detected amplitude, the TX S11 at the carrier (VNA port 1 at the SMA reference plane, to catch near-field loading or detuning), the optional RX S11 (the port 2 reflection on a two-port VNA) and the ambient baseline.
  5. Order: NEAR TX, MIDWAY, NEAR RX, then the reverse, so slow drift cancels.
  6. Repeat with W1 tool steel (annealed) rods in the same holes and stations, with the same full scan, then on a second day (and, if time allows, at r + λ/4 to move the standing-wave phase).
  7. Fit each station with calculator F and compare κ across stations and between brass and W1.

6. Caged rod fixture configuration (proposal)

Added 8 Oct 2026 at Dan's request. A proposal and a test of a hypothesis, not a result. Nothing here has been built or measured.

What the rods are for on this bench. The rod scan tests the SLW hypothesis: a scalar-longitudinal wave with its electric field along the TX to RX axis would be attenuated most when the rods lie along that axis (φ = 0 in T = exp(-κ cos²φ), with φ the angle between the wire direction and the propagation direction). The ordinary-wave reading of the same curve is in section 4, as the alternative to rule out.

hypothesis Idea. Run 2 puts the 2.5 in TX and RX spheres in their own closed cages. If an SLW passes through those cages, as the hypothesis supposes, then also enclosing the rod fixture and its lazy Susan in a third Faraday cage strips away any residual ordinary (transverse) leakage that still reaches the rods. A rod-angle pattern that survives this is much harder to attribute to that leakage.

Build notes

Numbers marked derived are plain arithmetic from λ = 691.42 mm at 433.59 MHz and the station table in section 5. They are guidelines, not measured shielding.

  • Turn the turntable without opening a gap. Best: a non-metallic shaft (acrylic, PTFE or Delrin) through a short metal tube bonded all round to the cage wall, with the angle dial on the outside end. The shaft must not be metal: a metal shaft through the tube turns it into a coaxial line, which passes RF at any frequency. Alternative: a hand crank on the same kind of shaft, Dan's string and drill method with the string through the same kind of small tube, or open the cage to turn and close it again before each reading (slower, and the closed state must be checked every time).
  • derived Size of the shaft hole. A round hole only passes waves above its TE11 cutoff, fc = 1.841 c / (π D). The cutoff diameter is about 405 mm at 433.59 MHz (and about 72 mm at 2.45 GHz, if the cage is ever reused on that bench). Use a hole far smaller: about 10 mm bore. Well below cutoff, a tube attenuates about 30 dB for each diameter of length in ideal theory, so a 10 mm bore tube at least 30 mm long gives roughly 90 dB on paper at either frequency. A plastic shaft filling the tube lowers the cutoff a little (by the square root of its permittivity), which changes nothing at these sizes. Real seams leak more than theory, which is why the cage is measured (below).
  • derived Keep the cage walls away from the rods. Use at least λ/(2π) = 110 mm between the walls and the swept rod envelope, the same rule the page uses for steel near the rods, and more where room allows (λ/2 = 346 mm is a comfortable target). With the array envelope radius of 244.5 mm (section 5), that means an inside width of at least about 0.71 m in the turning plane (about 1.18 m for λ/2 clearance), and the rod stack height (345.7 mm) plus 2 × 110 mm vertically, more if the fixed V array shares the cage.
  • derived Station fit. At r = 4λ, the NEAR TX and NEAR RX pivots (415.2 mm from a sphere centre) leave only about 60 mm between a 0.71 m wide fixture cage and the sphere centre, so they cannot be used with a caged fixture and caged spheres. Use MIDWAY (1382.8 mm), or lengthen r and recompute with calculator G.
  • derived The closed cage is a resonant box. A cube about 0.71 m inside has its lowest resonance near 300 MHz, so at 433.59 MHz the closed fixture cage supports standing waves. Any ordinary leakage that does get in can bounce around and depend on rod angle. Run 4 (rods out) and the cage shielding check are there to catch that.
  • Bond the seams. Overlap every mesh seam and the door or lid seam, and bond them continuously (conductive tape, clamped overlap or stitched seam). No cables through the fixture cage wall: nothing inside it needs power or signal.
  • Instruments in the caged runs. Section 3 rules out cables through a cage wall, so with each sphere in its own cage the source and receiver sit inside the sphere cages on battery, for example two TinySA units recording to microSD, as in section 8b.
  • Check each cage before the runs. Put a TinySA generator inside the cage on battery (MODE → SIGNAL GENERATOR, 433.59 MHz, -19 dBm) driving a small antenna, and a second TinySA with an antenna outside at a fixed distance. Read the level with the cage open, then closed. The difference is the shielding in dB. If the closed reading is at the noise floor, log it as "at least" that many dB. Repeat after any change to seams, the door or the shaft.
  • Settle step. The rod settle step still applies: after each 10° step wait about 3 s, or turn the shaft smoothly.
  • Log cage state in every row: cage_state for the sphere cages (open, or closed with layout), the new fixture_cage column (open, caged), material (brass, W1, none for rods out), and the latest shielding check in the notes.

7. Rod jolts and settle time

A bumped rod rings briefly, like a plucked ruler. Each rod is held at mid-length in the acrylic plate, so it behaves as two cantilevers. estimate The lowest bending mode is about 51 to 55 Hz for the 346 mm brass rods and about 76 to 82 Hz for W1. The shorter rods on the 1.3 and 2.45 GHz benches ring at a few hundred Hz to a few kHz. With an assumed Q of about 100, the long brass rods take about 2 s to settle. Working and assumptions: rod-jig page, section 7.

A possible small effect, not a proven problem. A rod that is still swinging moves slightly in the field, which could wobble the S21 or TinySA reading. Nobody has seen this on this bench yet. The one-minute flick test below tells you whether it matters on yours.

It is the jolts that matter, not turning versus stopping. Two methods are fine:

  • (a) Step and settle (recommended default): turn to each angle, take your hands off, wait about 3 s, then read. Log the wait as settle_s.
  • (b) Continuous: also fine if the motion is smooth, for example a slow motor drive or Dan's string and drill method. A reading or sweep that lasts longer than about 1 s averages out any small leftover ringing.

Avoid jerky hand turns, or a turntable with detent clicks, while a reading is being taken. The same settle step applies in the caged rod fixture configuration.

One-minute check. Watch the live S21 trace on the VNA (or the TinySA trace), lightly flick one rod, and see whether the trace wobbles. If it does not, rod jiggle is a non-issue and the settle time is just cheap insurance.

Step-by-step: run the flick test

Turning the lazy Susan smoothly: string and drill method

Dan's idea, 8 Oct 2026. A proposal, not yet tried on this bench.

Tie a string to the edge of the lazy Susan and let a cordless (battery) drill at low speed wind the string onto its chuck. The turntable then turns slowly, smoothly and continuously, which is method (b) above.

  • Why it is gentle. The string only pulls, so little drill vibration reaches the rods.
  • Wrap, do not just tie. For a full 360° scan, wrap the string a few turns around the rim (or a drum on the turntable) so it leaves the rim tangentially. A string tied at one point pulls at a changing angle, so the rate varies and it cannot pull past about half a turn.
  • Start and stop are the jolts. Start the drill before you start logging, and stop logging before you release the trigger. The start and stop are when the rods get kicked.
  • Stick-slip is the real jiggle risk. A cheap turntable bearing can stick and jump. Oil the bearing and add a slight constant drag, for example a felt pad rubbing on the underside, so the string stays taut and the motion stays even.
  • Drill noise. A brushed drill motor gives off broadband RF noise from its brushes. Keep the drill outside any cage, on battery, a few feet from the receiver. In the caged configuration, pass the string through a small bonded tube like the shaft tube (about 10 mm bore, at least 30 mm long). Plain nylon or cotton string carries no RF; do not use a string with a metal core or tinsel.
  • Angle against time. Log the time as each 30° mark on the dial passes (a phone video of the dial and the screen together works), then read the level against angle afterwards. Log turn_method = drill (or hand-step for the step and settle method).
  • derived Turn rate, formula only. Turntable speed (rev/min) = d × N / (2R), where d is the winding diameter on the chuck, N the drill speed in rev/min and R the radius where the string leaves the turntable. In degrees per second that is 3 × d × N / R. The winding diameter grows as string builds up on the chuck, so the rate creeps up during a scan; that is another reason to log the dial marks rather than assume a steady rate.

Two checks before using it for data, with the flick-test setup:

  1. Drill running, transmitter off. Always take this trace first, with the drill where it will be during the scan. Anything above the transmitter-off floor at 433.59 MHz is drill noise; move the drill farther away until it is gone.
  2. Drill running, no flick. Transmitter on, turntable turning, nobody touching anything. Run it first with the rods out (plates only), where nothing should change with angle, then with the rods in. If the trace is as flat as at rest (apart from the slow change with angle when the rods are in), the method is clean.

Why not simulate it? A full model of the 346 mm rod in Bob Greenyer's Standing Wave Project is too slender for the tool's in-browser grid (about 40 × 40 × 40 cells), so the live trace is the better check.

8. Flick test, step by step

Back to Rod jolts and settle time

Two ways to run it. Use whichever instrument you will use for the scan, or both. The rods, plates, spheres, range r and fixture station stay exactly as they will be for the real scan (MIDWAY is a good first station).

Flick tool: a wooden dowel or pencil about 30 cm long, so your hand and body stay out of the TX to RX path. Your body near the path changes S21 by itself, which is why the steps include a no-touch control.

8a. With the VNA6000-A (S21)

Menu paths below are the ones on the hub's VNA how-to, quoted from the NanoRFE VNA6000 user manual (2022/11). Firmware on your unit may differ.

  1. Calibrate. Recall the Exp B calibration (slot 0, 380 to 490 MHz): CAL → RECALL → RECALL 0. If there is none, do the SOLT calibration with cables, including THRU, as in VNA how-to, section 5. Check that a THRU reads about 0 dB, and that a C-number shows on the left of the screen.
  2. Connect port 1 to the TX ball and port 2 to the RX ball, with the same cables used for calibration. Tape the cables down.
  3. Single frequency. STIMULUS → CW FREQ, enter 433.59 MHz. This is the manual's zero span: one frequency sent continuously, so the trace shows S21 over time. (If you prefer to stay swept, use STIMULUS → CENTER 433.59 MHz and a narrow STIMULUS → SPAN, for example 1 MHz, and watch the marker.) Changing the range interpolates the calibration, which is fine here: this test looks at changes, not absolute values.
  4. Traces. DISPLAY → TRACE → TRACE 0, then DISPLAY → CHANNEL → CH1 THROUGH and DISPLAY → FORMAT → LOGMAG. Then DISPLAY → TRACE → TRACE 1, DISPLAY → CHANNEL → CH1 THROUGH and DISPLAY → FORMAT → PHASE.
  5. Zoom in. Use DISPLAY → SCALE to make the vertical scale fine (for example about 0.5 dB and about 5 degrees per division), and DISPLAY → SCALE → REFERENCE POSITION to bring both traces to mid-screen.
  6. Keep it sweeping. Make sure PAUSE SWEEP is not active, so the trace keeps updating.
  7. Baseline. Hands off and step back for 30 s. Note the normal jitter of the trace, peak to peak, in dB and in degrees. Take a screenshot (or a phone photo with the C-number and axis visible).
  8. Control: no touch. Bring the dowel up to a rod, hold it there for 2 s without touching, then withdraw it. Note any change. That is the stick and body effect alone.
  9. Flick. Lightly flick the free tip of one rod with the dowel (start with the centre rod, H5), withdraw the dowel at once and stand still. Watch both traces.
  10. Read it. Note the wobble peak to peak in dB and degrees, and how many seconds it takes to go flat again. A phone video of the screen makes the timing easy. The VNA probably refreshes too slowly to draw a 50 Hz ring as a clean wave (inference), so look for the trace turning ragged or jumping and then calming.
  11. Repeat three times, then with an outer rod, then with the W1 rods. Finally do one real 10° turntable step and watch the same way, since that is the jolt the scan actually makes.

8b. With TinySA Ultra+ ZS406 units (generator and receiver)

Menu names are from the official tinySA Ultra / Ultra+ menu tree and its linked menu-tree chart. Use two of the three ZS406 units: one as the source, one as the receiver.

  1. Source (TinySA #1). MODE → SIGNAL GENERATOR. Set FREQ to 433.59 MHz (sine output works up to 900 MHz on the Ultra+) and LEVEL to -19 dBm. Turn the output on with the LOW OUTPUT button. Connect it to the TX ball.
  2. Receiver (TinySA #2) on the RX ball, in MODE → SPECTRUM ANALYZER.
  3. Find the carrier. FREQUENCY → CENTER 433.59 MHz and FREQUENCY → SPAN 200 kHz. Then MARKER → SEARCH MARKER → PEAK SEARCH and note the marker frequency. The two units' clocks differ slightly, so the carrier may sit a little off 433.59 MHz.
  4. Zero span. FREQUENCY → ZERO SPAN and enter the marker frequency from step 3. The screen now shows level against time at that one frequency.
  5. RBW. FREQUENCY → RBW → 30kHz. That is wide enough to tolerate the small clock offset between the two units. Use 10kHz if the carrier is steady and you want a lower noise floor.
  6. Level. MARKER → MARKER OPS → ->REF LEVEL puts the carrier near the top. Then LEVEL → SCALE/DIV 1 dB. Leave LEVEL → LNA off unless the carrier is within about 10 dB of the noise.
  7. Time axis. DISPLAY → SWEEP TIME 1 s. With the default 290 points that is about 3.4 ms per point, fine enough to draw a 50 to 80 Hz ring directly on the trace. Make sure TRACE → CALC is OFF (no averaging, no hold), or the wobble will be smoothed away.
  8. Baseline, control and flick: as VNA steps 7 to 9. Note the normal jitter (dB peak to peak), the no-touch change, then the flick wobble and how long it takes to go flat.
  9. Freeze and save. DISPLAY → PAUSE SWEEP freezes a trace that shows the wobble. STORAGE → SAVE CAPTURE saves a screenshot and STORAGE → SAVE TRACES saves the trace as CSV to the microSD card. Turn PAUSE SWEEP off again to continue.
  10. Repeat as VNA step 11, including one real 10° turntable step.

What counts as "matters"

  • Does not matter: the flick wobble is no bigger than the baseline jitter, or no bigger than the no-touch control. Keep the 3 s settle as insurance and move on.
  • Handled by the settle time: the wobble is visible but has gone flat within 3 s. Keep 3 s (or the decay time you measured plus a margin).
  • Matters: the wobble is still there after 3 s, or it is larger than the effect you are trying to measure, for example the smallest step-to-step change in the angle scan you care about, or the allowed 0° versus 360° return-to-start difference. Then lengthen the settle time, use a smooth drive, or damp the rods (for example firmer O-rings), and repeat the test.

What to log (one row per flick): date_time, instrument (VNA with cal slot, or TinySA with RBW and sweep time), frequency, rod material and which rod, flick method, baseline jitter (dB and degrees, peak to peak), no-touch control change, flick wobble (dB and degrees, peak to peak), decay time to flat (s), the settle_s you chose, and the screenshot, CSV or video file name.
