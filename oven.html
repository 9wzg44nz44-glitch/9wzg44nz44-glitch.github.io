Round 1 · microwave oven experiment · draft v0.1

Microwave oven experiment

Use the 3/4 in sphere from Experiment A as a detector next to a 2.45 GHz microwave oven, and subject the oven's leakage to the same controls as Experiment B, including the polarising-rod fixture. The oven is its own transmitter; the sphere only listens.

Tests of hypotheses, not results. Mainstream electrodynamics treats free-space radio as transverse (TEM). A longitudinal or scalar component is a hypothesis we are testing, and nothing on these pages claims that scalar or longitudinal waves have been detected. Values marked proposal are our suggestions and are not measured or sourced. Draft v0.1, 4 Oct 2026, written by Grok Bot for Dan Britton.
Safety rules (read before any bench work).
  • Rods, spheres, probes, TinySA, cables and every other piece of metal stay outside the oven. Loose metal in a running oven can arc and damage the magnetron and the oven.
  • Never run the oven empty. Use a water load (a microwave-safe container with about 1 kg of water). Do not defeat or bypass the door interlocks and do not open or modify the oven.
  • Never connect the VNA, or any instrument input, to the oven antenna, waveguide or cavity. Receive only, from outside, through an attenuator.
  • The federal limit for new ovens is 5 mW/cm² at 5 cm (21 CFR 1030.10). Do not try to raise leakage. Keep people out of the line between the oven and the receiver and use timed runs. Stay clear of anyone with a pacemaker or similar device.
  • Protect the analyzer input: start with at least 20 dB of attenuation and a spectrum-analyzer maximum-input check against its manual. Increase sensitivity only after seeing the level.

1. Why an oven, and what a detection would mean

A household oven is a few hundred watts to over a kilowatt of 2.45 GHz power. Dan's hypothesis is that anything that makes a transverse wave also makes some longitudinal (SLW) component. The oven is the only source we have with enough power for effects that are not microwatts. The honest framing:

  • fact An oven leaks ordinary transverse 2.45 GHz radiation. A sphere, a dipole or any other antenna will receive it. Seeing a signal says nothing about SLW.
  • fact Within about λ/2π = 19.5 mm of a small source, and in the reactive region of a large aperture like a door seam, ordinary fields have a component along the line to the source. That is why the hub's near-field plan says the receiver must be well outside it.
  • hypothesis If an SLW component exists, the rod fixture, the TX-sphere rotation and the brass versus W1 swap would respond differently from a TEM wave (see Joule-loss predictions). The oven experiment asks whether the oven's leakage follows the same T(φ) behaviour as the sphere transmitters, and in what way it differs.

Questions: Q1 can the 3/4 in sphere launch and detect 2.45 GHz at all (Stage 1, exp-2450)? Q2 what does the oven's received signal look like with a sphere detector, and how does it vary with state, distance and angle (Stage 2)? Q3 does the oven, put through the Experiment B controls, behave like the sphere transmitter or differently?

3. Geometry

Schematic, not to scale. The oven door faces the receiver; the rod fixture stands on a non-metallic support between them; r is measured with a tape from the door plane to the sphere centre.

  • Notation. r = oven door to RX sphere centre distance. λ = 122.4 mm at 2450 MHz, λ/2 = 61.18 mm.
  • Stage 2a, near survey proposal: r from about 0.5 to 2 m with at least 20 dB of attenuation, to find where the signal is and how strong. In this zone the ordinary near field of the door seam is still mixed in, so these data are about signal strength and polarisation, not an SLW test.
  • Stage 2b, far test proposal following the hub's near-field plan: at least 4 m (one 2D²/λ for a 0.5 m door), with 10 to 12 m recommended, receiver inside Dan's Faraday cage. That is a long run in a house, so check the room layout and expect multipath from walls and floor (see range-finder).
  • Rod fixture. The 2.45 GHz fixture is the 4 x 4 x 1/4 in plate with a 3 x 3 array of 61.18 mm rods at 30.6 mm pitch (exp-2450). Place it between the oven and the sphere, with its centre on the line, at least λ/2π = 19.5 mm from the sphere and well away from the door seam. It has to sit on a non-metallic stand outside the closed door. Its position along the line is a variable: see section 6.

5. Measurement states and settings

  1. Baseline, oven off: room noise, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, the generator reference tone if used. Peak hold for 60 s.
  2. Oven on, 100% power, water load: timed runs of 30 s or less, with rests. Peak hold plus channel power across 2.40 to 2.50 GHz. The magnetron frequency moves with load and temperature, so a single bin is not reliable.
  3. Duty cycling (lower power settings): the magnetron turns on and off over a cycle of seconds, so use a time-domain (zero-span) trace if the analyzer supports it. proposal
  4. For each state record: date and time, oven make and model, power setting, water mass and temperature, r, rod material, angle, cage state (open or closed, grounded or not), attenuator, analyzer settings (RBW, detector, sweep) and the file name. The lab protocol and CSV log has a table format.

6. Three-position fixture scan at the oven (proposal)

Added 4 Oct 2026. Adapted from the sphere benches (exp-2450). Analysis: Joule-loss page, section 8. Checklist: To-Do group.

hypothesis Dan's early-attenuation idea, at the oven. The rotating fixture sits between the oven door and the receiving sphere. Placed close to the oven it should change the detected amplitude more than when it is midway or near the sphere, because paths that would reflect off objects between the two are attenuated before they can reflect. The closest point of approach of any rod during the rotation stays at least λ/(2π) = 19.5 mm from the door plane (and well away from the door seam). This is a hypothesis test and a proposal, not a result.

Where does the fixture go? The sources do not say. Monstein and Wesley (2002) report testing the wave by "intervening a cubic array of 9 half-wavelength wires" (abstract) and call it a polarizer-analyzer. They give no distance from either ball. Butterworth et al. (2013), describing that apparatus, write "Between Tx and Rx are positioned a pair of rotatable polarizer-analyzer arrays", which is where the word "between" comes from. Neither source fixes a position along the line, so this test treats the position as a variable to measure.

Protocol, with the oven safety rules kept

  1. Rods, stand and fixture stay outside the closed door on a non-metallic stand. Never put rods, the sphere, probes or any metal inside the oven. Use a water load (about 1 kg), 100% power, timed runs of 30 s or less with rests, and at least 20 dB of attenuation ahead of the analyzer.
  2. Fix the geometry: oven position and orientation, r, sphere height, cables, analyzer settings. Only the fixture moves, between NEAR OVEN, MIDWAY and NEAR SPHERE.
  3. Reference states at every station: oven off (ambient), oven on with the fixture removed, oven on with plates only.
  4. Angle scan at every station: φ = 0 to 180° in 10° steps, then once over 0 to 360°. Each angle needs its own timed run with a rest, so one scan is 19 runs; use a fresh or cooled water load per station and consider a 20° step for the first pass (proposal). Log the sphere-side level (peak hold and channel power across 2.40 to 2.50 GHz) for each run.
  5. Source loading check. There is no TX S11 here, and the VNA or any instrument input must never be connected to the oven. Use a fixed off-axis monitor (a second sphere or dipole that never moves, behind at least 20 dB of attenuation) and log its level and the magnetron peak frequency for every run. If either moves with the rod angle at NEAR OVEN, the fixture is loading the oven or pulling the magnetron. Optional: RX sphere S11 with the VNA, oven off only, to check the fixture is not detuning the sphere.
  6. Order and repeats: NEAR OVEN, MIDWAY, NEAR SPHERE, then reverse; brass, then W1 in the same holes; repeat on a second day.
  7. Fit each station with calculator F, pasting the monitor level into the third column.

The oven is not coherent with the instrument, so amplitude only. The magnetron frequency and output drift with load and temperature, which is why the fixed monitor matters: compare the sphere level to the monitor level, not to a fixed number. Log columns are those of the CSV template, with the monitor level in the tx_s11_dB column (rename it in your file). After each 10° rod step, wait about 3 s (or until the trace is flat) so the rods stop ringing, and log it as settle_s (rod-jig page, section 7).

7. Power balance, safely

The full write-up is on the Joule-loss page. In short: the oven's output is estimated by water calorimetry (P = m c ΔT / t); the leaked signal is measured only as a relative level through a fixed attenuator; the rod effect is the change in that level with angle and material. The leaked fraction of the magnetron output cannot be derived from these alone because the sphere is not a calibrated antenna.

8. Range finding at the oven

The oven is not coherent with the instrument, so the VNA-phase methods on the range-finder page do not apply. What remains: amplitude nulls versus position from ground and wall reflections (the "Two-ray nulls" tab of the calculator), dipole direction finding from two positions (direction-finding section), and the tape measure. As an assumed example with both antennas 1 m above a flat floor, the two-ray model puts the first nulls at 2.45 GHz near 32.7, 10.8, 6.4 and 4.5 m (Monstein and Wesley convention) or 16.3, 8.0, 5.3 and 3.8 m (ideal ground). The two conventions disagree, indoor floors and walls add more reflections, and so null positions are a cross-check to compare against the tape, not a stand-alone measurement. All range estimates here are hypotheses to test.

9. What would count against the idea

  • The oven's received level tracks the ordinary-wave picture: it falls with r roughly as 1/r in the far field, follows the TX orientation (rotating the oven or the TX sphere by 90° changes the curve) and is the same for brass and W1.
  • The rod curve is explained fully by the wire-grid control.
  • No difference between inside and outside the Faraday cage beyond ordinary shielding.
  • Results not repeatable between sessions beyond the quoted uncertainty. Repeat each scan at least twice, with the fixture re-seated.

A result that differs from these would be interesting, and would then need independent checks (a second oven, a second analyzer, a different room) before being believed.
